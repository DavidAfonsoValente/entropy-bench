\begin{abstract}
Base models are usually chosen by public benchmark scores, which vendors optimise for, which can
leak into training data, and which grade a model before the fine-tuning it will receive. We study a
label-free alternative: rank the candidates by how well they predict fresh, unlabelled text from the
target domain, in bits per byte. How the text is scored decides whether this works. Scored on whole
documents, each read from its own beginning, the ranking places all fifteen base models within one
rank of where they finish once fine-tuned on a news-writing task (Spearman $0.99$), against $0.97$
for HellaSwag and $0.59$--$0.71$ for GSM8K and MMLU-Pro. Scored the common way, on fixed-length
blocks cut from concatenated text, it misranks several models (Spearman $0.71$), and some of the
strongest models look weak. Adapting every model briefly to the domain repairs the block reading;
on whole documents the reading needs no adaptation. The
adapted ranking agrees with HellaSwag on general text, and on arXiv mathematics it agrees more
closely with GSM8K and MMLU-Pro than with HellaSwag. It is stable across adaptation seeds and
hardware. Code and artifacts are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Suppose an engineer has to pick a base model for a product. They have plenty of text from the
product's domain and a shortlist of a dozen open checkpoints, and the model they pick will be
fine-tuned and shipped. The most reliable way to choose would be to fine-tune every candidate and
compare the results, but that needs a task definition, labelled data and a training run for each
model. In practice the choice is made from benchmark scores instead: MMLU-Pro
\citep{wang2024mmlupro}, multiple-choice questions across academic subjects; HellaSwag
\citep{zellers2019hellaswag}, which asks for the most natural ending of a passage and is scored by
likelihood; and GSM8K \citep{cobbe2021gsm8k}, grade-school maths word problems. In this paper we show
that, on a news-writing task, the fine-tuning ranking can be anticipated before any task exists,
from unlabelled text alone. We rank the candidates by how well they predict held-out text from the
domain, scoring each document whole, from its own beginning. The resulting ranking matches the
ranking after fine-tuning almost exactly. Of the benchmarks, only HellaSwag comes close.

Benchmark scores can mislead for two reasons. The first is that benchmarks are public. They were
written for other purposes and released before the models they now evaluate, vendors optimise for
the ones they report, and their questions can end up in pre-training data. A high score may come
from a stronger model or from a model that has seen more of the test, and because pre-training data
is rarely disclosed, a reader cannot tell which. Benchmarks can also disagree sharply with how well
a model handles ordinary text. Llama-3.2-1B and Qwen-2.5-1.5B are $2.1$ points apart on HellaSwag
and their loss on news text after adaptation differs by only $3.4\%$, but they are $55.3$ points apart on GSM8K (accuracy, in percentage points).

The second reason is that models are rarely used as they ship. Teams fine-tune a base model on
their own data, so what matters is how good it becomes, and a benchmark score taken before that
can misjudge it. Loss on the domain's text can misjudge it too, depending on how the text is
scored. Gemma-4-12B is an example. Among the fifteen fine-tuned models it ranks ninth on GSM8K,
eighth on MMLU-Pro and thirteenth on loss scored the common way, on fixed-length blocks cut from
concatenated text. It ranks second on loss scored on whole documents, and it produces the best of
the fifteen fine-tuned systems (Table~\ref{tab:outofbox}).

Loss is a natural measure for this choice. There is no universal ground truth for which base model
is better: a benchmark tells us which model is better at that benchmark, and different benchmarks
disagree. A base model acquires its capabilities by minimising cross-entropy on text, and adaptation
minimises the same loss on the domain. Held-out loss is therefore the objective each candidate was
last trained on, measured on text it was not trained on. Cross-entropy is also a strictly proper
scoring rule \citep{gneiting2007scoring}. If $p$ is the distribution that generated the domain's
text and $q$ is a model, the model's expected loss is
\begin{equation}
H(p,q) = H(p) + \mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q),
\label{eq:ce}
\end{equation}
where $H(p)$, the entropy of the text, is the same for every model, and $\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q)$
measures how far the model's predictions are from the text's distribution \citep{cover2006elements}.
The difference between two models' expected losses is therefore the difference in their KL
divergence from the text, and held-out loss estimates it. Equation~\ref{eq:ce} says which model fits
the domain's text best; whether that predicts usefulness on a task is an empirical question, and
Section~\ref{sec:divergence} tests it on one task.

Three design choices keep the comparison fair. We count the loss per byte instead of per token,
because each model has its own tokenizer but they all read the same bytes; we call the result
\emph{bits per byte} (BPB). We compute it on fresh, decontaminated text that none of the candidates
has trained on. And we score each document whole, from the start the model was trained to see, on
exactly the same bytes for every model. We began with a different third choice: adapting every
candidate in the same way to the domain before scoring fixed-length blocks. That reading ranks the
fine-tuned models just as well, and we report both, because comparing them shows what adaptation
was correcting.

We evaluate seventeen base models from eight families, from 0.5B to 35B parameters. Eleven are
evaluated on four corpora (news, Reddit, Hacker News and arXiv mathematics), and fifteen are also
fine-tuned on a news task, writing an article's opening paragraph from its body.
Table~\ref{tab:cohort} lists which model enters which analysis. Our main findings are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{BPB on the domain's text anticipates fine-tuning on the task.} When the models are
ranked by BPB on whole documents before any fine-tuning, every one of the fifteen ends up within one
rank of its position after fine-tuning. Adapting the models first and scoring blocks does as well.
HellaSwag comes close, and GSM8K and MMLU-Pro are at chance (Figure~\ref{fig:downstream},
Section~\ref{sec:divergence}).
\item \textbf{How the text is cut can reorder the models.} Scored on blocks cut mid-document, some
strong models look weak, Gemma-4-12B most of all. Adaptation repairs this, and so does scoring each
document from its own start, with no adaptation (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).
\item \textbf{The choice of text changes what the ranking follows.} On general text the adapted
ranking agrees closely with HellaSwag; on arXiv mathematics it agrees more closely with GSM8K and
MMLU-Pro than with HellaSwag (Figure~\ref{fig:headline}B, Section~\ref{sec:agreement}).
\item \textbf{The ranking is stable.} No size-matched pair changes order across adaptation seeds,
and re-runs on other hardware reproduce the numbers (Section~\ref{sec:stability}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\textbf{Loss, compression and capability.} Language modelling was grounded in the entropy of text
from the start \citep{shannon1951prediction}. Token-level perplexity depends on each model's
vocabulary \citep{mielke2019perplexity,ahia2023languages}; normalising by UTF-8 bytes restores a
common unit \citep{gao2021pile,magnusson2023paloma}, and loss-based metrics carry more signal and
less noise than accuracy across hundreds of models \citep{heineman2025signal}. Language modelling
is equivalent to compression \citep{deletang2024compression}, and compression of fresh text
correlates linearly with benchmark scores across about thirty models
\citep{huang2024compression}. UncheatableEval \citep{uncheatableeval} maintains a leaderboard of
compression on recently published text for the same reason we use fresh text. These works, like our
main reading, measure models as they ship. We check the ranking against fine-tuned systems rather
than against benchmarks alone, and show that how the text is cut into evaluation windows can change
it. The metric is not new; the contribution is the protocol around it and what it shows.

\textbf{Choosing a model to fine-tune.} Lin et al.\ predict which LLM will fine-tune best by fitting
a rectified scaling law to small fine-tuning runs on the target task \citep{lin2024rectified}; this
needs task data, where our measurement needs only unlabelled text. Transferability estimators such
as LEEP and LogME \citep{nguyen2020leep,you2021logme} score a pre-trained model from its features on
labelled target data. Train-before-test \citep{zhang2025trainbeforetest} gives 61 models identical
supervised preparation across 24 tasks and raises cross-benchmark agreement; the idea of preparing
every candidate identically before comparing them is theirs, and we do it without labels, on fresh
text, in a unit that compares tokenizers. Observational scaling laws relate benchmark performance
across model families to a few capability measures \citep{ruan2024observational}, and loss predicts
downstream error within controlled families \citep{gadre2025scales}. Thrush et al.\ use the
varying correlation between per-domain perplexity and benchmark scores to select pre-training data
\citep{thrush2024perplexity}.

\textbf{Adapting before measuring.} Dynamic evaluation updates a model on the recent history of the
sequence it is being evaluated on \citep{krause2018dynamic}; domain-adaptive pre-training improves
fit to a target distribution \citep{gururangan2020dapt}. Our adapted reading adapts once, on a
training split, before any test text is scored.

\textbf{Contamination.} Models memorise duplicated pre-training text
\citep{carlini2021extracting,carlini2023quantifying}, so deduplication and contamination analysis
matter \citep{lee2022deduplicating,xu2024survey}. Reference-free membership signals
\citep{shi2024mink,zhang2025minkpp} are weak for large language models
\citep{duan2024mia}, which is why our main protection is the date of the text and the screens serve
only for conservative removal. Fresh benchmarks such as LiveBench \citep{white2025livebench} apply
the same idea to labelled questions.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:protocol}
The method has three phases (Figure~\ref{fig:protocol}). The corpus is collected and screened
before any model is run on it, then every candidate reads the held-out documents whole, and its
loss is counted in bits per byte. We also report a second reading, in which every candidate is
first adapted in the same way and then scored on fixed-length blocks. We scored both readings on
news; the adapted block reading is the one we have on all four corpora.

\paragraph{Phase 1: a decontaminated corpus.} The evaluation text must be text that no candidate
has seen, and we use three safeguards for this. \emph{Time:} Reddit and Hacker News were scraped in
August 2026, after every model in the cohort was released, and the arXiv titles and abstracts were
posted between 9 June and 20 August, after most of the releases. \emph{Hygiene:} we remove exact
duplicates, text that fails the Gopher quality heuristics \citep{rae2021gopher} and MinHash/LSH
near-duplicates \citep{broder1997resemblance} at Jaccard $0.60$. The 80/10/10 split shares no
identifier or normalised text across splits. \emph{Membership screens:} the news snapshot (Google
News, 8 June 2026, $119{,}054$ articles) predates some of the releases, so we also test every model
for having seen it. Min-K\%++ \citep{zhang2025minkpp} flags passages whose least likely tokens are
unusually likely for the model, and a context-sensitivity test flags passages whose loss drops more
than expected when the model is shown other text from the same corpus. We remove every passage
flagged for any model, $80$ in total, so all candidates are measured on the same corpus
(Appendix~\ref{app:robust}).

\paragraph{Phase 2: whole documents.} Each test document is read on its own, starting from its
tokenizer's beginning-of-document token, if it adds one, with up to $2{,}048$ tokens of
context. A document longer than that is read in overlapping windows with a stride of $1{,}024$, and
each token is scored once. The beginning-of-document token is
never scored. Every model reads the same first $6.0$\,MB of test documents, so the text is
identical byte for byte.

\paragraph{Phase 3: bits per byte.} Let $\loss$ be the total cross-entropy, in nats, over the
scored tokens of the test text, and $|B|$ the UTF-8 byte length of the text those tokens cover:
\begin{equation}
\bpb = \frac{\loss}{|B| \ln 2}.
\label{eq:bpb}
\end{equation}
A model with a larger vocabulary predicts fewer, longer tokens; dividing by bytes rather than tokens
removes the penalty per-token perplexity would give it.

\paragraph{The adapted block reading.} Every candidate is adapted to the retained training text,
packed into contiguous $512$-token blocks, using LoRA \citep{hu2022lora} on all linear layers, and
then scored on non-overlapping $512$-token test blocks cut from the packed test stream, each read
with no earlier context. Beginning-of-document tokens inside a block are used as context but never
scored. Because blocks are cut in tokens, each model covers a slightly different amount of text,
$5$--$10\%$ apart (Appendix~\ref{app:robust}). The same block reading without adaptation is the
\emph{unadapted block} reading. For adaptation, each candidate can get its own hyperparameter search with Optuna \citep{akiba2019optuna}.
A Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE; \citealp{bergstra2011tpe}) proposes the learning rate,
batch size, warm-up, weight decay, LoRA rank, $\alpha$ and dropout; successive halving
\citep{jamieson2016successive} prunes weak trials from $50$ steps on; and every trial is scored by
BPB on the validation split, never on the test split. Every model gets the same number of trials
and no time limit, because with a wall-clock limit small models complete more trials than large
ones and end up better tuned (Appendix~\ref{app:robust}). The best configuration is then trained
until validation BPB stops improving. The released pipeline implements this procedure. Every
adapted number in this paper uses a single shared configuration instead, for compute reasons
(Section~\ref{sec:setup}). Adapted models can also read whole documents, and we report that too.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
\paragraph{Models.} Eleven base checkpoints between 0.5B and 35B parameters are evaluated on all
four corpora (Table~\ref{tab:leaderboard}). Most are dense Transformers; one is a sparse
mixture-of-experts, and LiquidAI's LFM2.5-1.2B is a hybrid of convolution and attention blocks. For
the news corpus we add six more models, chosen before they were run to fill gaps in model size and
to add publishers, for 17 in total (Table~\ref{tab:cohort}). They come from the Llama, Qwen, Gemma,
Ministral, Mistral-NeMo, Falcon, SmolLM and LFM families
\citep{dubey2024llama3,meta2024llama32,yang2024qwen25,qwen2026qwen35,gemma2026gemma4,liu2026ministral3,mistral2024nemo,tii2024falcon3,benallal2025smollm2,liquid2026lfm25}.
Seven of the seventeen are Qwen models.

\paragraph{Corpora.} We use four corpora: news, Reddit, Hacker News and arXiv mathematics.
Table~\ref{tab:corpora} gives their sizes and how each was screened.

\paragraph{Adaptation runs.} Because of limited compute, all runs reported here use one shared
configuration instead of the per-model search: LoRA rank 16, $\alpha{=}32$, dropout $0.05$, learning
rate $10^{-4}$, effective batch size 32 and $250$ update steps, a common LoRA setting. The runs are a
fixed budget, not trained to convergence. This is $4.1$M training tokens per candidate and costs
$3.7$ GPU-minutes on one A100 at $0.5$B parameters, $11.6$ at $7.7$B, $24.7$ at $12$B and $62.7$ at
$31$B; at $512$ tokens per sequence every model's adaptation fits on one 64\,GB GPU. The budget is
matched in tokens, so models with larger vocabularies see a few percent more bytes of text. Its
results are stable across seeds and hardware (Section~\ref{sec:stability}).

\paragraph{Benchmarks.} For comparison, we score every model on MMLU-Pro (a stratified
$1{,}000$-question subset, so one question is $0.1$ points), HellaSwag (all $10{,}042$ validation
items) and GSM8K (all $1{,}319$ test items) with \emph{lm-evaluation-harness} 0.4.12
\citep{gao2024lmeval}, on one machine with one software build, following the protocol in
Appendix~\ref{app:rescore}. We use scores we measured ourselves, not the values reported on model
cards, and we publish the harness logs.

\paragraph{The fine-tuning task.} We fine-tune fifteen of the seventeen models
(Table~\ref{tab:cohort}), each starting from its base weights, to write an article's opening
paragraph, its lede, from the rest of the article. Gemma-4-31B and Qwen-3.5-35B-MoE are left out
because the fine-tune's $1{,}280$-token sequences and generation pass do not fit them on one GPU.
All fifteen use the same LoRA recipe as the adaptation and are scored by ROUGE-L, an F-measure based
on the longest common subsequence with the reference \citep{lin2004rouge}, on $500$ held-out
articles that are not part of the BPB test set. The pairs to compare and the statistic were fixed
in code before the fine-tuning jobs ran (Appendix~\ref{sec:downstream}). We chose this task because
every article provides its own reference, the lede written by the journalist, so no annotation is
needed, and writing a good lede requires reading the whole article and writing in the style of the
domain.

\paragraph{Statistics.} We compare rankings mainly by \emph{pairwise accuracy}, the fraction of
model pairs that a ranking orders in the same way as a reference. Pairwise accuracy counts only
\emph{size-matched} pairs, two models whose parameter counts differ by less than a factor of two;
when one model is more than twice the size of the other, the larger one usually wins, and a ranking
would get the pair right by following size. Because pairs that share a model are not independent,
$95\%$ intervals come from a cluster bootstrap over models ($20{,}000$ draws). Intervals for a single
fine-tuned pair resample its $500$ evaluation articles. With fifteen models the intervals are wide,
and we say so where it matters.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{BPB on the domain's text anticipates fine-tuning on the task}
\label{sec:divergence}
A team will fine-tune whichever model it chooses, so the practical question is whether the cheap
measurement can anticipate how that fine-tuning will turn out.
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_downstream.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{How well does each ranking predict the fine-tuned results?} Each point is a pair
of fine-tuned models whose parameter counts are within a factor of two ($39$ pairs; $38$ for GSM8K,
where two models tie). For each pair, $x$ is how strongly the ranking prefers one model (the gap in
that measure) and $y$ is how much better that model's fine-tuned system scores in ROUGE-L. Points
above the line are pairs the ranking orders correctly. Filled markers are \emph{resolved} pairs,
whose ROUGE-L difference has a $95\%$ bootstrap interval over the $500$ articles that excludes zero.
BPB on whole documents gets $37$ of $39$ pairs right, including $21$ of the $22$ resolved pairs,
and adapted block BPB, plotted here, orders the pairs identically; HellaSwag gets $34$; GSM8K gets
$18$ of $38$, and all eleven of its pairs with gaps between $15$ and $25$ points
fall below the line.}
\label{fig:downstream}
\end{figure}
Measured with no task, no labels and no training, BPB on whole documents orders $37$ of the $39$
size-matched pairs ($0.949$, $95\%$ interval $[0.817, 1.000]$) in the same way as the fine-tuned
systems. HellaSwag orders $34$ ($0.872$, $[0.640, 1.000]$), BPB on blocks without adaptation $30$
($0.769$), and GSM8K and MMLU-Pro are at chance with $0.474$ and $0.513$
(Figure~\ref{fig:downstream}, Table~\ref{tab:downstream}). The intervals of BPB and HellaSwag
overlap, so the data do not separate these two. On the $22$ pairs that the fine-tuning results
themselves resolve, the document reading gets $21$ right. Adapted block BPB, the reading we started
with, ranks the fifteen models in exactly the same order, so every ordering statistic in this
section is the same for both. Adapting the models and then reading whole documents orders $38$ of
the $39$ ($0.974$, $[0.862, 1.000]$) and all $22$ resolved pairs; Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} gives
the one pair it changes. One pair out of $39$, with intervals that do not separate the two
readings, does not pay for training every candidate, so the unadapted document reading is our main
one. Seven of the $39$ pairs differ by less than $2\%$ in adapted block BPB, inside the range we treat as a tie
(Section~\ref{sec:stability}); the band comes from the seed noise of adaptation, so it does not apply
to the document reading, which has none. Outside that band adapted block BPB orders $31$ of $32$, and its miss there
(Ministral-3-14B against Qwen-3.5-9B) is a pair the fine-tuned systems do not resolve. Its one
resolved miss, Mistral-Nemo-12B against Gemma-4-12B, is a $1.1\%$ gap inside the band. Over all
fifteen models the document ranking agrees with the final order at Spearman $0.99$: every model is
within one rank of its final position, and eleven are exactly in place. For comparison, unadapted
block BPB reaches $0.71$, GSM8K $0.59$, MMLU-Pro $0.71$ and HellaSwag $0.97$. BPB also beats ranking
by model size, by $+0.231$ in pairwise accuracy $[+0.059, +0.452]$.

A natural objection to the adapted reading is that the fine-tune reuses the adaptation's own
recipe, so a model that adapts quickly under it could win both. The objection does not apply to the
document reading, which involves no training. With a deliberately different fine-tuning recipe, recipe B (LoRA
rank $64$ and $\alpha{=}128$, three times the learning rate, half the batch size, $400$ steps and a new
seed), the order of the fine-tuned systems hardly changes (Spearman $0.982$). Document BPB and
adapted block BPB are still the best predictors at $0.923$, and they order correctly all $20$ pairs
that recipe B separates.

Since the adaptation and the fine-tuning both use news, one might suspect that news is just
predicting news. An exploratory check, run after the main result, suggests otherwise. For the nine
models that were both fine-tuned and adapted on all four corpora ($10$ size-matched pairs), BPB

\subsection{How the text is cut reorders the models}
\label{sec:mechanism}
The block reading without adaptation ranks the models differently, and adaptation changes that
order (Figure~\ref{fig:headline}A, Table~\ref{tab:ranks}). For the eleven models, the block rankings
before and after adaptation have a Spearman correlation of only $0.655$ on news, $0.609$ on Reddit
and $0.682$ on Hacker News, and one model moves seven places. The changes are uneven. On news,
adaptation lowers block loss by $3.6$--$5.4\%$ for every Qwen, Llama and Ministral model, but by
$17.0\%$ for Gemma-4-31B, $24.5\%$ for Gemma-4-12B and $36.4\%$ for LFM2.5-1.2B. These three are the
only models that move up, and every other model drops one or two places. Gemma-4-12B is ninth of
eleven before adaptation and second after; before adaptation it ranks below two models with fewer
than $2$B parameters. LFM2.5-1.2B is last before adaptation at $1.34$ bits per byte, behind a $0.5$B
Qwen at $0.93$, and ahead of it after adaptation.

Figure~\ref{fig:blockpos} shows where the block reading goes wrong. Before adaptation, Gemma-4-12B
starts a block at $2.83$ bits per byte, while Qwen-2.5-1.5B, eight times smaller, starts at $2.04$.
Gemma stays behind for most of the block and is ahead by the end, $0.72$ against $0.77$. The early
positions have the highest loss, so they contribute most of the difference between the two block
averages. Blocks are cut from a packed stream, so most start in the middle of a document, without
the beginning-of-document token that models such as Gemma are trained to see first.

Two tests, with the decision rule fixed before they ran, separate the cut from the model. First,
scoring only positions $64$, $128$ or $256$ onwards of each block removes the first tokens of the
block but not the mid-document start; on the $10$ size-matched fine-tuned pairs whose models we
scored this way, it leaves unadapted block BPB at $6$ correct, unchanged, against $9$ for adapted
block BPB. Second, we read every test document whole, as in Section~\ref{sec:protocol}. Without any
adaptation this ranks the fifteen fine-tuned models in exactly the order adapted block BPB does
(Spearman $1.00$), and Gemma-4-12B moves from thirteenth of fifteen to second. Most of what
adaptation did to the block reading was teaching the models to read text that starts mid-document.
We did not separate the beginning-of-document token, the document boundary and the longer context;
together, they amount to reading the text as the model was trained to see it.

On whole documents, adaptation still lowers every model's loss, by $2.0$--$7.9\%$, but it hardly
changes the order (Spearman $0.993$ between the two document readings). Gemma-4-12B again gains
the most, and its $7.9\%$ moves it past Mistral-Nemo-12B into first place, which is the one change
and the one resolved pair it corrects. The control models reproduce the published block readings on
the new host: unadapted to within $0.04\%$, and adapted to within $0.7\%$, inside the largest gap seed noise can open
(Section~\ref{sec:stability}).

\subsection{The choice of text changes what the ranking follows}
\label{sec:agreement}
This section and the next use the adapted block reading, the one we ran on all four corpora. On
news it orders the fifteen fine-tuned models exactly as the document reading does (Spearman
$1.00$); whether the document reading follows the same corpus dependence is untested.

Of the three benchmarks, HellaSwag is the closest to language modelling. It asks which ending of a
passage is most natural, and the harness scores each ending by its likelihood. Before adaptation, BPB
on news, Reddit and Hacker News has a Spearman correlation of $0.63$--$0.72$ with HellaSwag across the
eleven models; after adaptation it is $0.96$--$0.98$. On news, the adapted ranking orders $53$ of the
$55$ model pairs the same way as HellaSwag, counting all pairs and not only size-matched ones
(Table~\ref{tab:alignment} in Appendix~\ref{app:alignment}). Since both HellaSwag and BPB are based
on likelihood, some agreement is expected; adaptation is what makes it close. On general text,
adaptation also lowers the correlation with GSM8K on all three corpora and with MMLU-Pro on two.

When the models are adapted on arXiv mathematics instead, agreement rises with all three benchmarks:
with GSM8K from $0.76$ to $0.91$, with MMLU-Pro from $0.74$ to $0.93$, and with HellaSwag from $0.48$
to $0.82$. The rise is therefore not specific to the reasoning benchmarks. What changes is which
benchmark the ranking sits closest to: arXiv mathematics is the only corpus on which the adapted
ranking agrees more with GSM8K and MMLU-Pro than with HellaSwag, and ranking by mathematics BPB
selects GSM8K's top model. This shift is suggestive rather than established. A difference in
differences that tests it directly is positive in $93.7\%$ of model resamples (one-sided
$p \approx 0.06$) and in $80.9\%$ when whole publisher families are resampled ($p \approx 0.19$), and
it depends on the two Gemma models (Appendix~\ref{app:robust}).

\subsection{BPB rankings are stable}
\label{sec:stability}
A ranking is only useful if it is repeatable. The document reading involves no randomness: the
adapted jobs re-read every unadapted model and reproduced its document BPB exactly. The adapted
reading depends on the adaptation's seed, so we ran three seed replicates of all $17$ news
adaptations; the seed sets the adapter's initialisation, dropout and batch order, and the data
splits and test set stay fixed. None of the $40$ size-matched pairs changes order across seeds. The
median standard deviation of adapted BPB within a model is $0.0001$, against $0.074$ between models.
The largest gap seed noise could open between two runs is $1.24\%$ of the median BPB ($2\sqrt{2}$ standard deviations of the noisiest model, Gemma-4-12B; the median model, under $0.04\%$), so we treat a gap under $2\%$ as a tie. Re-running one model, Ministral-3-14B, on different hardware weeks later
reproduced unadapted BPB exactly and adapted BPB to within $0.0003$, and repeating a run with a
different software stack reproduced it to within $0.01\%$. Rankings also carry over across general
text: adapted rankings agree at $\rho=0.991$ between news and Reddit, $0.955$ between news and
Hacker News and $0.964$ between Reddit and Hacker News (Table~\ref{tab:crosscorpus} and
Figure~\ref{fig:crosscorpus}, Appendix~\ref{app:robust}). The unadapted ranking is just as
consistent across these corpora ($0.982$, $0.964$ and $0.973$). One might expect that adaptation buys stability, and it does not: it produces a different consistent ranking, the one that anticipates the
fine-tuned order in Section~\ref{sec:divergence}.

BPB has a structural advantage over generative benchmarks here. Floating-point addition is not
associative, so results on a GPU can change with batch size and kernel choice
\citep{he2025nondeterminism}. A generative benchmark decodes greedily, so a tiny change in two
nearly tied logits can flip a token, and the model then continues from its own changed output.
BPB is computed with teacher forcing: every prediction is conditioned on the true text, so a small
perturbation changes one log-probability slightly and does not propagate, and it is averaged over
about $1.3$ million token predictions. Generative scores are also sensitive to decoding settings that
BPB does not use: dropping a single decoding setting shipped with Gemma-4-12B changed its GSM8K
score by $9.86$ points and its MMLU-Pro score by $14.30$ (Appendix~\ref{app:rescore}). On hardware
alone the effect is small: the same weights move by $-0.60$ to $+0.69$ GSM8K points between an A100
and an H100, within the benchmark's sampling error (each of its $1{,}319$ questions is worth $0.076$ points).

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
We test one downstream task, lede writing in news, with two fine-tuning recipes; other task types
and domains may behave differently. The document reading was run on news only, for the fifteen
fine-tuned models, and on one host; the results across corpora, seeds and hardware are for the
adapted block reading. The cohort has fifteen fine-tuned models, seven of them Qwen, so
the intervals are wide and BPB and HellaSwag are not separated. The two largest models were
not fine-tuned, so the top of the ranking is not checked against a fine-tuned system. All
adapted runs use one shared adaptation recipe rather than a per-model search.
The shift towards GSM8K and MMLU-Pro under mathematics adaptation rests mainly on two Gemma models.
Membership screens cannot prove that a proprietary training set excludes a text; the dates of the
Reddit, Hacker News and arXiv corpora are the stronger guarantee.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:using}
Public benchmarks are a convenient way to choose a base model, but they are public, so they can be
optimised for and can leak into training data, and they grade a model before the adaptation it will
receive. We ranked candidates instead by their loss, in bits per byte, on fresh, decontaminated
text from the target domain, with each document read whole from its own start. It needs no labels,
no task definition and no training, only a forward pass over a few megabytes of text.

On a news-writing task, this ranking placed all fifteen fine-tuned systems within one rank of where
they finished (Spearman $0.99$), against $0.59$--$0.71$ for GSM8K and MMLU-Pro and $0.97$ for
HellaSwag. How the text is scored matters as much as which text. Cut into fixed-length blocks, the
same text misranks several models, and adapting every model first repairs that reading. Gemma-4-12B is thirteenth of fifteen on blocks, second on whole documents, and
produces the best of the fifteen fine-tuned systems. The choice of text changes which benchmark the
adapted ranking resembles: HellaSwag on general text (Spearman $0.96$--$0.98$), GSM8K and MMLU-Pro on mathematics. The ranking is
repeatable across seeds and hardware. A new corpus can be collected for every domain and every
evaluation, so the text it is measured on stays unseen.

\paragraph{Why the search gives every model the same number of trials.} The search of Phase~2 uses
a multivariate TPE sampler (seed $42$) over learning rate ($10^{-6}$--$10^{-3}$, log), physical batch
($2$, $4$, $8$), gradient accumulation ($1$--$16$), warm-up ratio ($0$--$0.2$), weight decay
($10^{-5}$--$10^{-1}$, log), LoRA rank ($8$--$64$), $\alpha$ as a multiple of rank ($1$, $2$, $4$) and
dropout ($0$--$0.2$); a successive-halving pruner starts trials at $50$ steps and promotes the best
third at each rung; and the objective is BPB on a fixed $256$-block subset of the validation split.
An earlier run of this search on the eleven-model news cohort was capped by wall-clock time rather
than by trial count, and the cap was confounded with model size: the two largest models completed
four trials each while the 1B models completed 17 and 18 (\texttt{results/legacy\_sweep/*/trials.csv}),
and the five models of 9B or more all kept the same third draw of the seeded sampler as their winner.
A time-capped search hands small models a better-tuned adapter and biases the ranking by parameter
count, which is why the released pipeline budgets the search in trials and applies no time cap by
default. Apart from the right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:blockpos}, none of those adapters produces a
number in the paper.

\paragraph{Membership screens.} The news corpus passes three reference-free membership screens per
model: Min-K\%++ (normalised likelihood of the lowest-probability $K{=}20\%$ target tokens),
context sensitivity (target loss with and without sampled same-corpus prefixes) and, for flagged
passages only, preference for the original passage over two word-swap perturbations. The union over
models and screens removed $80$ unique sequences, after which every model receives the identical
retained corpus. Near-$100\%$ preference for the original is expected from fluency alone, so we draw
no memorisation conclusion from the third test (\texttt{results/contamination/}).

\paragraph{Context length.} Varying the inference context on $64$ fixed held-out target windows from
$16$ to $2048$ tokens, both models measured improve with context and the larger improves faster, so
a protocol that scores only short contexts under-rates models that use long ones. Measured context
sensitivity is $0.032$ bits per byte per doubling, the constant the bound below uses. The document
reading of Section~\ref{sec:protocol} scores up to $2{,}048$ tokens of context.

\paragraph{Token-aligned blocks: a small bias, bounded.} Evaluation blocks are 512 \emph{tokens}
rather than a fixed byte span, so models with different tokenizers score slightly different amounts
of text: 5.92--6.24\,MB across the eleven-model cohort, a $5\%$ spread, and $9.9\%$ across the
seventeen. Bounding the resulting bias with the measured context sensitivity puts it at $0.08\times$
the smallest adjacent rank gap in the eleven-model cohort. In the extended cohort the worst ratio of
bound to gap is $0.61$, for Qwen2.5-3B over Falcon3-7B; the bound overturns no rank. The document
reading covers identical bytes for every model and removes the question.

\paragraph{Seed variation is negligible.} The published adaptation cells did not fix the \texttt{torch} random
seed, which sets the LoRA initialisation, dropout and batch order (the data splits are seeded
separately). A check fixed before it ran re-ran all $17$ news models with three explicit seeds ($51$
cells, everything else pinned). The median within-model standard deviation of adapted BPB is
$0.0001$ against a between-model standard deviation of $0.074$, and none of the $40$ pairs of models
within $2\times$ in size reverses its order across seeds. Gemma-4-12B is the one model where seed
noise is visible (standard deviation $0.003$, five times the next largest).

\paragraph{The mathematics shift.} Adapting on general text raises agreement with HellaSwag on news
from $\rho = 0.718$ to $0.982$ while GSM8K falls from $0.800$ to $0.700$ and MMLU-Pro from $0.845$ to
$0.773$; adapting on arXiv mathematics raises GSM8K agreement to $0.909$ and recovers GSM8K's top
model, while the three general-text corpora all leave GSM8K at or below its unadapted agreement. The
shift predicts a \emph{joint} movement, so we test it with a difference in differences: (GSM8K minus
HellaSwag selection accuracy under mathematics adaptation) minus the same quantity under a
general-text corpus, which is $+0.242$ pooled. Resampling models gives a strictly positive difference
in $93.7\%$ of draws (one-sided $p \approx 0.06$). Resampling whole publisher families, which the
shared training data of a family calls for, gives $80.9\%$ ($p \approx 0.19$), with ties counted as
non-positive; not one of its $20{,}000$ draws is negative. A leave-one-family-out jackknife shows the
dependence: dropping the two Gemma models takes the pooled difference from $+0.242$ to $+0.056$.
More families, rather than more models, would settle it.

\paragraph{Reproduction controls.} Re-running two Ministral-3-14B cells weeks later on different
hardware reproduced unadapted BPB exactly and adapted BPB to within $0.0003$. Because the six added
models were adapted on a different software stack, a published cell (Qwen2.5-7B) was re-run through
that stack; the cohorts are pooled only because it reproduces within $1\%$, and it reproduces to
$0.01\%$ on adapted BPB ($0.7169$ against $0.7170$).

\section{The Downstream Fine-Tune}
\label{sec:downstream}
Section~\ref{sec:divergence} reports the result; this section describes how it was built. It ran in
two stages, each fixed in code before its job returned: first the two pairs on which the rankings
disagree most (Gemma-4-12B against Ministral-3-14B, Llama-3.2-1B against Qwen-2.5-1.5B), then every
cohort model that fits one GPU, so that no pair is hand-picked. Gemma-4-31B and Qwen-3.5-35B-MoE need
more than one GPU for this fine-tune, because its $1{,}280$-token sequences and its generation pass
cost far more memory than the $512$-token adaptation (Section~\ref{sec:setup}), and are excluded by
that rule, which costs one size-matched pair of forty.

\paragraph{The task.} Journalistic convention makes a news article's first paragraph a standalone
summary of it, so the corpus supplies its own supervision: the input is the article with its opening
paragraph removed, truncated to $3{,}000$ characters, and the target is that paragraph. Articles
qualify if they have at least three paragraphs, an opening paragraph of $200$--$700$ characters
ending in sentence punctuation, and at least $800$ characters of remaining body. Of the $11{,}905$
validation documents, $3{,}627$ qualify; $2{,}000$ are sampled for training and $500$ for evaluation
under a fixed seed.

\paragraph{Why the validation split.} The BPB test blocks live in the \emph{test} split, so the
fine-tuning task is built from the validation split instead. Evaluation documents are hashed against
the test documents twice, on full normalised text and on a $400$-character opening, the second
because a syndicated wire story is republished with an edited body and an identical lede. Four
candidate articles were dropped by that second check, before the $500$ were sampled, and none by the
first.

\paragraph{Equal preparation.} Every model gets the adaptation recipe unchanged (LoRA rank $16$,
$\alpha{=}32$, dropout $0.05$, learning rate $10^{-4}$, effective batch $32$, $250$ steps), starting
from its base weights, with prompt tokens masked out of the loss so the model is trained to write
the lede rather than to reconstruct the article. The tokenizers differ, so the budget is matched in
update steps $\times$ batch $\times$ sequence length; each model's realised token count is in the
artifact. Decoding is greedy with a $180$-token cap and each checkpoint's own generation
configuration otherwise untouched, following Appendix~\ref{app:rescore}. The prompt is plain text:
the article body followed by an instruction to write its opening paragraph. When the prompt is too
long, the body is cut, never the instruction after it, because cutting the assembled prompt would
delete the instruction from the longest articles, at a different article count for each tokenizer.
At most four of the $500$ evaluation articles were cut for any model, and none for three of them.

\paragraph{The decision rules, fixed before each run.} For the two chosen pairs: mean ROUGE-L F1
over the $500$ held-out articles, a paired bootstrap over articles ($20{,}000$ draws, $95\%$ percentile
interval) and a practical-equivalence margin of $0.01$; an interval excluding zero resolves the pair
for whichever ranking predicted that winner. The first pair resolved for adapted BPB; the second was
inconclusive. For the full cohort the fine-tuned ROUGE-L is the outcome, and every ranking is scored
by pairwise accuracy within $2\times$ with a model-level bootstrap of $20{,}000$ draws
(Table~\ref{tab:downstream}). A separate guard voids any comparison in which fine-tuning failed to
improve a model over its own base weights, since ordering unmoved models would order their base
behaviour under another name. It did not fire: all fifteen models gained between $+0.041$ and
$+0.153$.

\paragraph{A second recipe.} Recipe B changes every setting the adaptation and the fine-tune shared
(LoRA rank $64$, $\alpha{=}128$, learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$, effective batch $16$, $400$ steps, a
new seed) on the same task, data, decoding and metric, and was fixed before it ran. It fits the
training articles far harder and scores slightly below recipe A for every model, yet orders the
fifteen systems almost identically (Spearman $0.982$). Against recipe-B systems adapted block BPB,
and document BPB with the same order, remain the top ranking at $0.923$ $[0.750, 1.000]$, ahead of HellaSwag at $0.846$, parameter count at $0.744$
and GSM8K and MMLU-Pro near chance, and it picks the better system on all $20$ pairs recipe B
resolves. Its margin over parameter count, resolved under recipe A, does not resolve here ($+0.179$
$[-0.036, +0.441]$), so that margin is recipe-specific (Table~\ref{tab:downstream}).

\paragraph{What copying scores.} A news body restates its own lede, so reference overlap is partly
available by copying. The article's first body sentence scores $0.125$ against the reference and a
random body sentence $0.111$. Unadapted base weights land close to copying (inspection confirms they
largely do copy), while every fine-tuned system clears it, from $0.188$ (Qwen-2.5-0.5B) to $0.249$
(Gemma-4-12B).

\paragraph{How close the corpus has to be.} Exploratory, run after the result: on the ten
size-matched pairs of the nine fine-tuned models that have all four corpora, adapted BPB orders the
news fine-tune with pairwise accuracy $0.900$ for news and for Reddit, $0.700$ for Hacker News and
$0.600$ for arXiv mathematics; the unadapted block readings reach $0.500$--$0.600$. Ten pairs support no interval, so this is a direction, not a result.

\section{The Benchmark Re-Score}
\label{app:rescore}
\paragraph{Harness configuration.} MMLU-Pro is 5-shot chain-of-thought with exact match on one
stratified $1{,}000$-question subset (seed 42); HellaSwag is 10-shot length-normalised
multiple-choice accuracy on all $10{,}042$ validation examples; GSM8K is 5-shot chain-of-thought with
flexible numeric extraction on all $1{,}319$ test examples. All bf16, greedy, with each tokenizer's
own beginning-of-sequence token.

\paragraph{One host.} Generative scores do not pool across hosts: identical released weights move
$-0.60$ to $+0.69$ GSM8K points when only the GPU changes from A100 to H100. The benchmark columns were therefore scored again in full on one host
(A100-SXM-64GB) with one software build: seventeen models $\times$ three benchmarks $=$ fifty-one
cells, with the design and the exclusion rule fixed in advance. The task definitions match the
earlier scoring exactly (identical task hashes for all seventeen models on GSM8K and HellaSwag; MMLU-Pro ships none), and
every overlapping cell agrees with it to within $1.3$ points, most to within $0.3$.

\paragraph{Decoding settings.} The newer harness honours each checkpoint's shipped
\texttt{generation\_config.json}, and the earlier one did not. Replacing every checkpoint's
generation settings with one identical policy also discarded \texttt{suppress\_tokens}, which is set
for Gemma-4-12B; that omission cost it $9.86$ GSM8K and $14.30$ MMLU-Pro points, while HellaSwag,
scored by likelihood, was unchanged. Keeping each checkpoint's own settings for all seventeen models
reproduces the earlier scoring for Gemma-4-12B to within $+1.21$ points on GSM8K, $+0.10$ on MMLU-Pro
and $-0.27$ on HellaSwag. The GSM8K cell is the one outside the $1.0$-point tolerance fixed in
advance, within the allowance of one such cell.
