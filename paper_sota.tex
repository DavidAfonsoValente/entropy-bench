\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
Base models are usually chosen by public benchmark scores. Vendors optimise for those scores, their
items can leak into training data, and they grade a model before the fine-tuning it will receive. We
show that a short, unlabelled adaptation to the target domain predicts how models will rank after
fine-tuning, before any task data exist. Every candidate is adapted the same way to fresh text from the
domain and ranked by how well it then predicts held-out text. This takes minutes to an hour per model
on one GPU. Ranked this way, fifteen base models land within one rank of where they finish once
fine-tuned on a news-writing task. We also show that adaptation reorders the models, lifting some
that look weak before adaptation to the top, and that the adapted ranking, with no fine-tuning,
agrees with the benchmarks built for the domain. Adapted on general web text and news, it converges
on HellaSwag, the likelihood-scored benchmark closest to language modelling; adapted on arXiv
mathematics, it moves towards the reasoning benchmarks GSM8K and MMLU-Pro. The ranking is stable
across adaptation seeds and hardware and requires no labelled data. Code and artifacts are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Suppose an engineer has to pick a base model for a product. They have plenty of text from the
product's domain and a shortlist of a dozen open checkpoints, and the model they pick will be
fine-tuned and shipped. The most reliable way to choose would be to fine-tune every candidate and
compare the results, but that needs a task definition, labelled data and a training run for each
model. In practice the choice is made from benchmark scores instead: MMLU-Pro, a set of knowledge
questions; HellaSwag, which asks for the most natural ending of a passage and is scored by
likelihood; and GSM8K, grade-school maths. In this paper we show that the fine-tuning ranking can be
predicted before any task exists, from unlabelled text alone. We adapt every candidate in the same
way to text from the domain and then rank the candidates by how well they predict held-out text
from it. Since the text can be collected fresh for each domain, we call this a \emph{dynamic
evaluation}. The resulting ranking matches the ranking after fine-tuning almost exactly. Of the
benchmarks, only HellaSwag comes close.

Benchmark scores are misleading for two reasons. The first is that benchmarks are public. They were
written for other purposes and released before the models they now evaluate, vendors optimise for
the ones they report, and their questions can end up in pre-training data. A high score may come
from a stronger model or from a model that has seen more of the test, and because pre-training data
is rarely disclosed, a reader cannot tell which. Benchmarks can also disagree sharply with how well
a model handles ordinary text. Llama-3.2-1B and Qwen-2.5-1.5B are
$2.1$ points apart on HellaSwag and their loss on news text after adaptation differs by only
$3.4\%$, but they are $55.3$ points apart on GSM8K (accuracy, in percentage points).

The second reason is that models are rarely used as they ship. Teams adapt a base model to their
own data first, so what matters is how good it becomes after adaptation. A model that has seen
little text like the target domain can look weak at first and still end up ahead. Gemma-4-12B is an
example: among the fifteen fine-tuned models it ranks ninth on GSM8K, eighth on MMLU-Pro and
thirteenth on loss before adaptation, second on loss after adaptation, and it produces the best of
the fifteen fine-tuned systems.

We argue that loss is the right measure. There is no universal ground truth for which base model is
better: a benchmark tells us which model is better at that benchmark, and different benchmarks
disagree. A base model learns what it can do by minimising cross-entropy on text, and adaptation
minimises the same loss on the domain. Held-out loss is therefore the objective each candidate was
last trained on, measured on text it was not trained on. Cross-entropy is also a proper scoring
rule. If $p$ is the distribution that generated the domain's text and $q$ is a model, the model's
expected loss is
\begin{equation}
H(p,q) = H(p) + \mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q),
\label{eq:ce}
\end{equation}
where $H(p)$, the entropy of the text, is the same for every model, and $\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q)$
measures how far the model's predictions are from the text's distribution. The difference between
two models' expected losses is therefore the difference in their KL divergence from the text, and
held-out loss estimates it \cite{shannon1951prediction}.

Three design choices keep the comparison fair. We count the loss per byte instead of per token,
because each model has its own tokenizer but they all read the same bytes; we call the result
\emph{bits per byte} (BPB). We compute it on fresh, decontaminated text that none of the candidates
has trained on. And we give every candidate the same adaptation procedure on the same text, so that
each model is evaluated in the state it would be used in. Train-before-test
\cite{zhang2025trainbeforetest} also prepares all candidates identically before comparing them, but
it uses labelled benchmark data. Our approach needs no labels, uses fresh text and measures in
bytes.

We evaluate seventeen base models from eight families, from 0.5B to 35B parameters, on four
corpora: news, Reddit, Hacker News and arXiv mathematics. Fifteen of the models are also fine-tuned
on a news task, writing an article's opening paragraph from its body. Our main findings are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{Adapting to the domain predicts fine-tuning on the task.} When the models are ranked
by adapted BPB before any fine-tuning, every model ends up within one rank of its position after
fine-tuning. HellaSwag comes close and the reasoning benchmarks do not. Adapting on Reddit instead
of news predicts the news fine-tune equally well, so the prediction does not depend on adapting to
the task's own text (Figure~\ref{fig:downstream}, Section~\ref{sec:divergence}).
\item \textbf{Adaptation reorders the models.} Loss before adaptation partly reflects how familiar
a model already is with the style of the text. Adaptation removes most of that effect, and some
models that look weak beforehand move to the top (Figure~\ref{fig:headline}A,
Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).
\item \textbf{The choice of text determines what the ranking measures.} On general text the
adapted ranking agrees closely with HellaSwag; on mathematics it agrees with the reasoning
benchmarks GSM8K and MMLU-Pro (Figure~\ref{fig:headline}B, Section~\ref{sec:agreement}).
\item \textbf{The ranking is stable.} It barely changes across adaptation seeds, hardware and
general-text corpora, while benchmark scores for the same weights shift when only the evaluation
machine changes (Section~\ref{sec:stability}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Method}
\label{sec:protocol}
The method has three phases (Figure~\ref{fig:protocol}). The corpus is collected and screened
before any model is run on it, then every candidate is adapted in the same way, and finally each
adapted model is scored on held-out text.

\label{sec:phases}

\paragraph{Phase 1: a decontaminated corpus.} The evaluation text must be text that no candidate
has seen, and we use three safeguards for this. \emph{Time:} the text is dated after the candidates'
release. Reddit and Hacker News were scraped in August 2026, after every model in the cohort was
released, and the arXiv titles and abstracts run from 9 June to 20 August. \emph{Hygiene:} we remove
exact duplicates, text that fails the Gopher quality heuristics \cite{rae2021gopher} and MinHash/LSH
near-duplicates at Jaccard $0.60$. The 80/10/10 split shares no identifier or normalised text across
splits, so nothing in the test split reappears in training. \emph{Membership screens:} the news
snapshot (Google News, 8 June 2026, $119{,}054$ articles) predates some of the releases, so we also
test every model for having seen it. Three reference-free tests look for traces of memorisation.
Min-K++ \cite{zhang2024minkpp} flags passages whose least likely tokens are unusually likely for the
model. A context-sensitivity test flags passages whose loss drops more than expected when the model
is shown other text from the same corpus. A perturbation test checks whether the model prefers the
original passage over copies with swapped words. We remove every passage flagged for any model,
$80$ in total, so all candidates are measured on the same corpus (Appendix~\ref{app:robust}).

\paragraph{Phase 2: equal adaptation.} Every candidate is adapted to the retained training text,
packed into contiguous $512$-token blocks, using LoRA \cite{hu2021lora} on all linear layers. In the
full method each candidate gets its own hyperparameter search with Optuna \cite{akiba2019optuna}. A
Tree-structured Parzen Estimator proposes the learning rate, batch size, warm-up, weight decay, LoRA
rank, $\alpha$ and dropout; successive halving prunes weak trials from $50$ steps on; and every trial
is scored by BPB on the validation split, never on the test split. Every model gets the same number
of trials and no time limit. With a wall-clock limit, small models complete more trials than large
ones and end up better tuned, a bias we observed in an earlier run (Appendix~\ref{app:robust}). The
best configuration is then trained until validation BPB stops improving. The released pipeline
implements this procedure (\texttt{-{}-hparams sweep -{}-no-time-limit}).


\paragraph{Phase 3: bits per byte.} Cross-entropy on the held-out test split is divided by the
UTF-8 byte length $|B|$ of the text those predictions cover:
\begin{equation}
\bpb = \frac{\loss_{\text{nats}}}{\ln 2} \times \frac{|\text{Tokens}|}{|B|}
\label{eq:bpb}
\end{equation}
The byte count depends only on the text, so it is the same for every candidate, and a larger
vocabulary gives no advantage as it would with per-token perplexity. Test blocks do not overlap,
and each starts with no earlier context. Beginning-of-document tokens between packed documents are
used as context but never scored. We report BPB both before adaptation, which we call unadapted (or
zero-shot) BPB, and after adaptation, since the difference between the two is informative
(Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).



\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
\paragraph{Models.} We evaluate 11 base checkpoints between 0.5B and 35B parameters on all four
corpora (Table~\ref{tab:leaderboard}). Most are dense Transformers; one is a sparse
mixture-of-experts and one, LiquidAI's LFM2.5-1.2B, is not a Transformer. For the news corpus we add
six more models, chosen in advance to fill gaps in model size and to add publishers, for 17 in total
(Table~\ref{tab:cohort}). They come from the Llama, Qwen, Gemma, Ministral, LiquidAI, Falcon,
Mistral and SmolLM families
\cite{dubey2024llama3,yang2024qwen25,gemma2026gemma4,liu2026ministral3,qwen2026qwen35}.

\paragraph{Corpora.} We use four corpora: news, Reddit, Hacker News and arXiv mathematics.
Table~\ref{tab:corpora} gives their sizes and how each was screened.

\paragraph{Adaptation runs.} Because of limited compute, all runs reported here use a single
hand-picked configuration instead of the per-model search: LoRA rank 16, $\alpha{=}32$, dropout
$0.05$, learning rate $10^{-4}$, effective batch size 32 and $250$ update steps. This is $4.1$M
training tokens per candidate and costs $3.7$ GPU-minutes on one A100 at $0.5$B parameters, $11.6$ at
$7.7$B, $24.7$ at $12$B and $62.7$ at $31$B. The configuration is close to what our earlier search
found (rank 16 won for eight of the eleven models, and the median winning learning rate was
$1.1\times10^{-4}$), and its results are stable across seeds and hardware
(Section~\ref{sec:stability}). Adaptation ran on Leonardo (CINECA), on 3 nodes with 4 A100 64\,GB
GPUs each.

\paragraph{Benchmarks.} For comparison, we score every model on MMLU-Pro \cite{wang2024mmlupro},
HellaSwag \cite{zellers2019hellaswag} and GSM8K \cite{cobbe2021gsm8k} with
\emph{lm-evaluation-harness} 0.4.12 \cite{gao2024lmeval}, on one machine with one software build,
following the protocol in Appendix~\ref{app:rescore}. We use scores we measured ourselves, not the
values reported on model cards, and we publish the harness logs so every score can be recomputed.

\paragraph{The fine-tuning task.} We fine-tune fifteen of the seventeen models
(Table~\ref{tab:cohort}) to write an article's opening paragraph, its lede, from the rest of the
article. Gemma-4-31B and Qwen-3.5-35B-MoE are left out because they do not fit on one GPU. All
fifteen use the same LoRA recipe and are scored by ROUGE-L on $500$ held-out articles that are not
part of the BPB test set. The analysis was written before the jobs ran
(Appendix~\ref{sec:downstream}). We chose this task because every article provides its own
reference, the lede written by the journalist, so no annotation is needed, and writing a good lede
requires reading the whole article and writing in the style of the domain. ROUGE-L, the longest
common subsequence shared with the reference, is the standard automatic score for this kind of
summarisation \cite{lin2004rouge}.

\paragraph{Statistics.} We compare rankings mainly by \emph{pairwise accuracy}, the fraction of
model pairs that a ranking orders in the same way as a reference. Its $95\%$ intervals come from a
bootstrap over models rather than over pairs, because pairs that share a model are not independent
($20{,}000$ draws). Intervals for a single fine-tuned pair resample its $500$ evaluation articles.
Pairwise accuracy only counts \emph{size-matched} pairs, meaning two models whose parameter counts
differ by less than a factor of two. When one model is more than twice the size of the other, the
larger one usually wins, and a ranking would get the pair right just by following size.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Adapting to the domain predicts fine-tuning on the task}
\label{sec:divergence}
A team will fine-tune whichever model it chooses, so the practical question is whether the cheap
measurement can predict how that fine-tuning will turn out.
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_downstream.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{How well does each ranking predict the fine-tuned results?} Each point is a pair
of fine-tuned models whose parameter counts are within a factor of two ($39$ pairs; $38$ for GSM8K,
where two models tie). For each pair, $x$ is how strongly the ranking prefers one model (the gap in
that measure) and $y$ is how much better that model's fine-tuned system scores in ROUGE-L. Points
above the line are pairs the ranking orders correctly. Filled markers are \emph{resolved} pairs,
whose ROUGE-L difference has a $95\%$ bootstrap interval over the $500$ articles that excludes zero.
Adapted BPB gets $37$ of $39$ pairs right, including $21$ of the $22$ resolved pairs; HellaSwag gets
$34$; GSM8K gets $18$ of $38$, and all eleven of its pairs with gaps between $15$ and $25$ points
fall below the line.}
\label{fig:downstream}
\end{figure}
Measured with no task and no labels, adapted BPB orders $0.949$ of the $39$ size-matched pairs in
the same way as the fine-tuned systems ($95\%$ interval $[0.817, 1.000]$). HellaSwag orders $0.872$
of them, while GSM8K and MMLU-Pro are at chance level with $0.474$ and $0.513$
(Figure~\ref{fig:downstream}, Table~\ref{tab:downstream}). On the resolved pairs, which the
fine-tuning results themselves separate, adapted BPB gets $21$ of the $22$ right. Over all fifteen
models its ranking agrees with the final order at Spearman $0.99$: every model is within
one rank of its final position, and eleven are exactly in place. For comparison, unadapted BPB
reaches $0.71$, GSM8K $0.59$, MMLU-Pro $0.71$ and HellaSwag $0.97$. Under this recipe adapted BPB
also beats ranking by model size, by $+0.231$ in pairwise accuracy $[+0.059, +0.452]$. With a
deliberately different fine-tuning recipe, recipe B (four times the LoRA rank, three times the
learning rate, half the batch size and a new seed), the order of the fine-tuned systems hardly
changes (Spearman $0.982$). Adapted BPB is still the best predictor at $0.923$ and orders all $20$
pairs that recipe B separates correctly.

Since the adaptation and the fine-tuning both use news, one might suspect that news is just
predicting news. This is not the case. For the nine models that were both fine-tuned and adapted on
all four corpora, BPB adapted on Reddit predicts the news fine-tuning as well as news BPB does
(Spearman $0.983$ each). Hacker News reaches $0.933$, while the same corpora without adaptation reach
only about $0.6$. The prediction comes from adapting to fresh general text, not from adapting to the
task's own domain. arXiv mathematics, which shifts the ranking towards reasoning
(Section~\ref{sec:agreement}), predicts the news fine-tuning less well. A few minutes of unlabelled
adaptation therefore capture most of what fine-tuning on the task reveals, while the reasoning
benchmarks capture little of it.

Gemma-4-12B, ninth on GSM8K and eighth on MMLU-Pro among the fifteen models, produces the best
fine-tuned system, ahead of Ministral-3-14B by $+0.018$ ROUGE-L $[+0.011, +0.025]$. The $55.3$-point
GSM8K gap between Llama-3.2-1B and Qwen-2.5-1.5B makes no measurable difference after fine-tuning.
Every model improved on its own base weights and beat the score of simply copying the article's
first sentence (ROUGE-L $0.125$).

\subsection{Adaptation reorders the models}
\label{sec:mechanism}
Adaptation lowers every model's loss, but it also changes their order
(Figure~\ref{fig:headline}A, Table~\ref{tab:ranks}). For the eleven models, the rankings before and
after adaptation have a Spearman correlation of only $0.655$ on news, $0.609$ on Reddit and $0.682$ on
Hacker News, and one model moves seven places. The changes are uneven. On news, adaptation lowers
loss by $3.6$--$5.4\%$ for every Qwen, Llama and Ministral model, but by $17.0\%$ for Gemma-4-31B,
$24.5\%$ for Gemma-4-12B and $36.4\%$ for LFM2.5-1.2B. These three are the only models that move up,
and every other model drops one or two places. Gemma-4-12B is ninth of eleven before adaptation and
second after; before adaptation it ranks below two models with fewer than $2$B parameters.
LFM2.5-1.2B, the only non-Transformer, is last before adaptation at $1.34$ bits per byte, behind a
$0.5$B Qwen at $0.93$, and ahead of it after adaptation.

We attribute this to familiarity. Loss before adaptation mixes two things: whether a model already
writes like the corpus, and whether it is a capable model. Figure~\ref{fig:blockpos} separates them
by plotting loss against position inside an evaluation block. Each block is read with no earlier
context, so the model starts every block cold. Before adaptation, Gemma-4-12B starts a block at
$2.83$ bits per byte, while Qwen-2.5-1.5B, eight times smaller, starts at $2.04$. Gemma stays behind
for roughly the first half of the block and is ahead by the end, $0.72$ against $0.77$. An average
over the block therefore mostly measures how quickly a model gets used to unfamiliar text, such as
the formatting, document conventions and register of this corpus. That is a real property, but a
model choice should not depend on it. A short adaptation, the same for every model, provides this
familiarity. The loss reduction it brings is largest at the start of the block but remains after it,
so what is left is closer to capability. Unadapted loss is still meaningful, but it measures
something else, namely how similar the model already is to the target text. It penalises most the
models whose pre-training text differs most from the corpus, which is also why the non-Transformer,
with its own training mix, is affected most.

\subsection{The choice of text determines what the ranking measures}
\label{sec:agreement}
Of the three benchmarks, HellaSwag is the closest to language modelling. It asks which ending of a
passage is most natural, and the harness scores each ending by its likelihood. Before adaptation, BPB
on news, Reddit and Hacker News has a Spearman correlation of $0.63$--$0.72$ with HellaSwag across the
eleven models; after adaptation it is $0.96$--$0.98$. On news, the adapted ranking orders $53$ of the $55$ model pairs the same way as HellaSwag, counting all pairs and not only size-matched
ones (Table~\ref{tab:ranks}; the full matrix is Table~\ref{tab:alignment} in
Appendix~\ref{app:alignment}). Three independently collected corpora reach the same order once
familiarity is removed, and it is the order of the benchmark closest to what BPB measures. Since both
HellaSwag and BPB are based on likelihood, this agreement is expected. It shows that measuring on
one's own fresh text loses nothing that HellaSwag captures, without the problems of a public
benchmark.

The result depends on what the text is about. On general text, adaptation lowers the correlation
with GSM8K on all three corpora and with MMLU-Pro on two of them. When the models are adapted on
arXiv mathematics instead, BPB moves towards the reasoning benchmarks, with Spearman correlations of
$0.91$ with GSM8K and $0.93$ with MMLU-Pro, up from $0.76$ and $0.74$, and ranking by mathematics BPB
selects GSM8K's top model. With news the measurement agrees with the language benchmark, and with
mathematics it agrees with the reasoning benchmarks. A benchmark cannot be redirected like this
because its content is fixed, whereas the corpus is chosen by whoever is choosing the model.



\subsection{Adapted BPB rankings are stable}
\label{sec:stability}
A ranking is only useful if it is repeatable. We ran three preregistered seed replicates of all
$17$ news runs; the seed sets the adapter's initialisation and dropout, and the data splits and
test set stay fixed. The median standard deviation of adapted BPB within a model is $0.000087$, against a
spread of $0.074340$ between models, a ratio of $0.0012$, and none of the $40$ size-matched pairs
changes order across seeds. The only model with visible seed noise is Gemma-4-12B, which varies by up
to $1.24\%$, so gaps below $2\%$ are best treated as ties. Re-running two models on different
hardware reproduced unadapted BPB exactly and adapted BPB to within $0.0003$, and repeating a run with
a different software stack reproduced it to within $0.01\%$. The ranking also carries over across
domains: adapted rankings agree at $\rho=0.991$ between news and Reddit, $0.955$ between news and
Hacker News and $0.964$ between Reddit and Hacker News (Table~\ref{tab:crosscorpus} and
Figure~\ref{fig:crosscorpus}, Appendix~\ref{app:robust}). The unadapted ranking is just as stable
across these corpora ($0.982$, $0.964$ and $0.973$), so adaptation does not make the ranking more
stable. It produces a different stable ranking, the one explained in Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} and
shown in Section~\ref{sec:agreement} to agree with HellaSwag. Only mathematics differs from the other
corpora, and only after adaptation: its agreement with the general corpora drops from $0.91$--$0.97$
unadapted to $0.74$--$0.84$ adapted, because adapting on mathematics brings out a different
capability.

Benchmark scores are less stable. The same released weights move by $-0.60$ to $+0.69$ GSM8K points
when only the GPU changes from A100 to H100, and one decoding default shipped with a checkpoint was
worth $9.86$ GSM8K and $14.30$ MMLU-Pro points for Gemma-4-12B in our re-score
(Appendix~\ref{app:rescore}). The reason lies in how the two are computed. Both run on hardware with
the same floating-point nondeterminism, since GPU reductions are not associative and their results
can change with batch size and kernel choice \cite{he2025nondeterminism}. A generative benchmark
decodes greedily, so a tiny change in two nearly tied logits can flip a token. The model then
continues from its own changed output, and over a chain-of-thought answer the difference can grow
until it flips a right-or-wrong verdict; each of GSM8K's $1{,}319$ questions is worth $0.076$ points.
BPB is computed with teacher forcing: every prediction is conditioned on the true text, not on the
model's earlier outputs, so a small perturbation changes one log-probability slightly and does not
propagate. It is also averaged over about $1.3$ million token predictions on news. Decoding settings,
which changed Gemma-4-12B's GSM8K score by ten points, play no role because nothing is generated. A
generative benchmark score depends on the model, the harness, the decoding configuration and the
hardware; BPB on a fixed corpus after a fixed adaptation depends mainly on the model.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:using}
Base models are usually chosen with public benchmarks, which have three problems. They are
\emph{expensive to run well}: a generative benchmark has to decode every answer, and its score
changes with the hardware and the decoding settings, while the evaluation a team really needs,
fine-tuning every candidate on its task, costs a training run per model. They \emph{do not reflect
deployment}, since they test a model as it ships while teams first adapt it to their own text. And
they are \emph{contaminated}: they are public, vendors optimise for them, and their questions can
end up in pre-training data.

We propose a dynamic evaluation that addresses all three. Every candidate is adapted in the same way
to fresh, decontaminated text from the target domain and ranked by its held-out loss in bits per
byte. This is the loss each candidate is adapted to minimise, measured per byte so that models with
different tokenizers can be compared.

\textbf{It is cheap.} Adaptation takes minutes to an hour per model on one GPU and needs no labels,
yet it predicts the outcome of the expensive evaluation. All fifteen fine-tuned systems finish
within one rank of the position adapted BPB gave them (Spearman $0.99$), compared with
$0.59$--$0.71$ for the reasoning benchmarks, and adapting on Reddit instead of news predicts the news
fine-tuning equally well. The full method tunes each model separately; because of limited compute,
the runs here share one recipe, and a very different fine-tuning recipe leaves the order of the
systems at Spearman $0.982$.

\textbf{It reflects deployment.} Each model is measured after adaptation, on the kind of text it will
be used on. Adaptation removes the advantage of already knowing the text's style and changes the
ranking: Gemma-4-12B is ninth of eleven before adaptation, second after, and produces the best of
the fifteen fine-tuned systems. The choice of text decides what the ranking measures. On general
text it agrees with HellaSwag (Spearman $0.96$--$0.98$), and on mathematics with GSM8K and MMLU-Pro
($0.91$ and $0.93$).

\textbf{It is clean.} The text is dated after the models' release or screened for membership, and
deduplicated, so no model has seen it. A new corpus can be collected for every domain and every
evaluation.

\textbf{It is stable.} Because BPB is computed with teacher forcing, it is repeatable: seed-to-seed
variation is about a thousandth of the spread between models. A different seed moves adapted BPB by
up to $1.24\%$ (Gemma-4-12B; the median model, $0.01\%$), so we treat a gap under $2\%$ as a tie. The
same weights, by contrast, move by up to $0.69$ GSM8K points when only the hardware changes.

Our experiments cover seventeen base models, one fine-tuning task and one shared recipe per stage.
Post-trained checkpoints can be evaluated in the same way. The released benchmark keeps one
leaderboard per corpus and recipe: a news track that we run, and a mathematics track on arXiv titles
and abstracts, whose corpus is public.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\textbf{From perplexity to comparable loss.} Language modelling was grounded in byte- and
character-level entropy from the start \cite{shannon1951prediction}, but token-level perplexity
depends on each model's vocabulary \cite{mielke2019perplexity,ahia2023languages}; normalising by
UTF-8 bytes restores a common unit \cite{gao2020pile,magnusson2023paloma}, and loss-based metrics
carry better signal and lower noise than accuracy across 465 models \cite{heineman2025signal}. Our
contribution is the operational protocol around the metric, not the metric.

\textbf{Where the loss is measured matters.} Thrush et al.\ turn the varying correlation between
per-domain perplexity and benchmark performance into a pretraining-data selection rule
\cite{thrush2024perplexity}. Their estimand is the complement of ours: they fix the benchmark suite
and choose data to \emph{train} one strong model, where we fix the candidate pool and ask how the
\emph{selection ranking} moves.

\textbf{Equal preparation.} Dynamic evaluation updates a model on recent test history
\cite{krause2018dynamic,krause2019dynamictransformer} and domain-adaptive pretraining improves fit to
a target distribution \cite{gururangan2020dapt}. Closest to our motivation, \emph{train-before-test}
gives 61 models identical supervised preparation across 24 tasks and raises cross-benchmark Kendall
agreement from $0.52$ to $0.76$ \cite{zhang2025trainbeforetest}; they rank post-fine-tuning accuracy
on public labelled benchmarks, where we rank held-out fit after \emph{unsupervised} adaptation to a
fresh corpus, across tokenizers. The core idea, prepare every candidate identically before
comparing them, is theirs; what we add is doing it without labels, on text no vendor has seen, in a
unit that compares tokenizers, and checking the result against real fine-tuned systems. Controlled
families show a smooth loss-to-error relation
\cite{gadre2025scales,ruan2024observational}; we test it \emph{across} families.

\textbf{Contamination.} Models memorise duplicated pretraining text
\cite{carlini2021extracting,carlini2022quantifying}, so deduplication and contamination analysis are
necessary \cite{lee2022deduplicating,xu2024survey}; reference-free membership signals are useful
screens \cite{shi2023mink,zhang2024minkpp} but prove nothing about a proprietary training set, so we
use them only for conservative quarantine.

\paragraph{Stability across domains.}\label{sec:domains} All eleven models ran on three independently collected domains under the same recipe
(Tables~\ref{tab:corpora} and~\ref{tab:leaderboard}). Adaptation genuinely changes the
choice: unadapted and adapted rankings agree only at Spearman $\rho = 0.655$ on news, $0.609$ on
Reddit and $0.682$ on Hacker News, with models moving up to seven places, and the adapted ordering
then holds across domains: news and Reddit agree on nine of eleven exact ranks
($\rho = 0.991$, maximum displacement one), news against Hacker News gives $0.955$ and Reddit
against Hacker News $0.964$, and the agreement is not merely ordinal (Figure~\ref{fig:crosscorpus}).

\paragraph{Why the search gives every model the same number of trials.} The search of
Phase~2 uses a multivariate TPE sampler (seed $42$) over learning rate ($10^{-6}$--$10^{-3}$, log),
physical batch ($2$, $4$, $8$), gradient accumulation ($1$--$16$), warm-up ratio ($0$--$0.2$), weight
decay ($10^{-5}$--$10^{-1}$, log), LoRA rank ($8$--$64$), $\alpha$ as a multiple of rank ($1$, $2$,
$4$) and dropout ($0$--$0.2$); a successive-halving pruner starts trials at $50$ steps and promotes the
best third at each rung; and the objective is BPB on a fixed $256$-block subset of the validation
split. An earlier run of this search on the eleven-model news cohort was capped by wall-clock time
rather than by trial count, and the cap was confounded with model size: the two largest models
completed four trials each while the 1B models completed 17 and 18
(\texttt{results/legacy\_sweep/*/trials.csv}), and the five models of 9B or more all kept the same
third draw of the seeded sampler as their winner, while the smaller models' winners came from much
later in their searches. A time-capped search hands small models a better-tuned adapter and biases
the ranking by parameter count, which is why the released pipeline budgets the search in trials and
applies no time cap by default. Apart from the right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:blockpos}, which uses three of its adapters to show
where in a block adaptation gains, none of those adapters produces a number in the paper; the
reported results use the hand-picked configuration of Phase~2.

\paragraph{Membership screens.} The news corpus additionally passes three reference-free membership screens
per model: Min-K++ (normalised likelihood of the lowest-probability $K{=}20\%$ target tokens),
context sensitivity (target NLL with and without sampled same-corpus prefixes), and, for flagged
candidates only, preference over two word-swap/placeholder perturbations. The global union
quarantined $80$ unique sequences, after which every model receives the identical retained corpus.
Near-$100\%$ preference for the unperturbed original is expected (fluency alone produces it), so we
draw no memorisation conclusion from it (\texttt{results/contamination/}).

\paragraph{The cold start, seen a second way.} Varying the inference context on $64$ fixed held-out
target windows from $16$ to $2048$ tokens reproduces Figure~\ref{fig:blockpos}'s effect
non-positionally: both models improve with context and the larger improves faster, so any protocol
that scores short contexts under-rates the models that use long ones. Measured context sensitivity is
$0.032$ bits per byte per doubling, which is the constant the block-alignment bound below uses.

\paragraph{Token-aligned blocks: a real bias, bounded.} Evaluation blocks are 512 \emph{tokens}
rather than a fixed byte span, so models with different tokenizers score slightly different amounts
of text: 5.92--6.24\,MB across the eleven-model cohort, a $5\%$ spread, and $9.9\%$ across the
seventeen. Bounding the resulting bias with the measured context sensitivity puts it at
$0.08\times$ the smallest adjacent rank gap in the eleven-model cohort. In the extended cohort the
bound is looser and worth stating: on the worst adjacent pair, Qwen2.5-3B over Falcon3-7B, whose BPB
gap is only $0.29\%$, the bound reaches $0.61\times$ the gap. It still \textbf{overturns no rank},
but byte-aligned blocking would remove the question, and we recommend it for future work.

\paragraph{Seed variation is negligible.} Every adaptation cell ran with the \texttt{torch} RNG
unseeded, so a preregistered check re-ran all $17$ news models three times ($51$ cells, everything
else pinned). Median within-model SD of adapted BPB is $0.000087$ against a between-model SD of
$0.074340$, a ratio of $0.0012$, and \emph{no} pair of models within $2\times$ in size ($0$ of
$40$) reverses its ordering across seeds. The spread is not uniform: Gemma-4-12B is the one model where seed noise is
visible at all (SD $0.00315$, $5.3\times$ the next-largest spread).

\paragraph{Steering: direction, not magnitude.} Adapting on general text raises agreement with
HellaSwag on news from $\rho = 0.718$ to $0.982$ while GSM8K falls from $0.800$ to $0.700$ and
MMLU-Pro from $0.845$ to $0.773$; adapting on contamination-screened arXiv mathematics instead
raises GSM8K agreement to $0.909$ and restores the GSM8K oracle pick at zero regret,
while three general-text corpora tried as controls all leave GSM8K at or below the do-nothing
baseline. That is the rotation, and it is why the body reports only its direction. Because the
steering hypothesis predicts a
\emph{joint} movement, the better-powered statistic is a difference in differences: (GSM8K minus
HellaSwag selection accuracy under mathematics adaptation) minus the same quantity under a
general-text corpus, which is $+0.242$ pooled. Resampling models gives a strictly positive
difference in $93.7\%$ of draws; the stricter publisher-family clustering the mechanism itself
implies gives $80.9\%$ with not one negative draw in $20{,}000$, but no longer clears $0.05$ on any
tie convention. A leave-one-family-out jackknife shows why: dropping the two Gemma models takes the
pooled difference from $+0.242$ to $+0.056$. The remedy is more \emph{families}, not more models.

\paragraph{Three reproduction controls.} Re-running two Ministral-3-14B cells weeks later on
different hardware reproduced zero-shot BPB exactly and adapted BPB to within $0.0003$, two orders of
magnitude below the differences compared. Because the extension models were adapted on a different
software stack, a published cell (Qwen2.5-7B) was re-run through the extension pipeline; the analysis
refuses to pool the cohorts unless it reproduces within $1\%$, and it reproduces to $0.01\%$ on
adapted BPB ($0.7169$ against $0.7170$). And because benchmark rankings are themselves estimates,
recomputing selection accuracy while discarding pairs whose benchmark gap is smaller than $k$
combined standard errors leaves the result intact and slightly improves it: on GSM8K $0.909$ at
$k{=}0$ becomes $0.933$ at $k{=}3$, and on HellaSwag $0.836$ becomes $0.863$, so the metric's errors
concentrate in pairs the benchmark itself cannot separate.

Section~\ref{sec:divergence} reports the result; this is how it was built, and what it does and
does not license. It ran in two stages, each preregistered in code before its job returned: first the
two pairs on which the selectors disagree most (Gemma-4-12B against Ministral-3-14B, Llama-3.2-1B
against Qwen-2.5-1.5B), then every cohort model that fits one GPU, so that no pair is chosen by
anyone. Gemma-4-31B and Qwen-3.5-35B-MoE need more than one GPU \emph{for this fine-tune} (its
$1{,}280$-token sequences and its generation pass cost far more memory than the $512$-token
adaptation priced in Section~\ref{sec:intro}) and are excluded by that rule, which costs one in-band
pair of forty.

\paragraph{The task.} Journalistic convention makes a news article's first paragraph a standalone
summary of it, so the corpus supplies its own supervision: the input is the article with its opening
paragraph removed, truncated to $3{,}000$ characters, and the target is that paragraph. Articles
qualify if they have at least three paragraphs, an opening paragraph of $200$--$700$ characters
ending in sentence punctuation, and at least $800$ characters of remaining body. Of the $11{,}905$
validation documents, $3{,}627$ qualify; $2{,}000$ are sampled for training and $500$ for evaluation
under a fixed seed.

\paragraph{Why the validation split.} The BPB test blocks live in the \emph{test} split, so the
fine-tuning task is built from the validation split instead. That is verified rather than assumed: the
test split reproduces from an independent reconstruction, and the adaptation pipeline's contamination pass (which would have split the corpus differently) is off by
default and recorded as off in every published cell. Evaluation documents are then hashed against the
test documents twice, on full normalised text and on a $400$-character opening, the second because a
syndicated wire story is republished with an edited body and an identical lede. Four articles were
dropped by that second check and none by the first.

\paragraph{Equal preparation.} Both members of a pair get the paper's own adaptation recipe unchanged
(LoRA rank $16$, $\alpha{=}32$, dropout $0.05$, learning rate $10^{-4}$, effective batch $32$, $250$
steps) with prompt tokens masked out of the loss so the model is trained to write the lede rather
than to reconstruct the article. Nothing is tuned on either model, because a per-model search is
exactly the cost this protocol exists to avoid. The tokenizers differ, so the budget is matched in
update steps $\times$ batch $\times$ sequence length; each model's realised token count is in the
artifact. Decoding is greedy with a $180$-token cap and each checkpoint's own generation
configuration otherwise untouched, following Appendix~\ref{app:rescore}. The prompt is assembled
so that the instruction always survives truncation: the \emph{body} is cut to fit, never the
instruction after it, because right-truncating the assembled prompt would delete the instruction from
the longest articles at a different article count for each tokenizer: an asymmetry between the two
models being compared. At most four of the $500$ evaluation articles were cut for any model, and
none for three of them.

\paragraph{The decision rules, fixed before each run.} For the two chosen pairs: mean ROUGE-L F1
over the $500$ held-out articles, a paired bootstrap over articles ($20{,}000$ draws, $95\%$ percentile
interval) and a practical-equivalence margin of $0.01$; an interval excluding zero resolves the pair
for whichever reading predicted that winner. The first pair resolved for adapted BPB; the second was
inconclusive. For the full cohort: the fine-tuned ROUGE-L is the outcome and every selector is scored with the paper's own statistic: pairwise accuracy within $2\times$, model-level
cluster bootstrap, $20{,}000$ draws (Table~\ref{tab:downstream}). A separate guard voids any
comparison in which fine-tuning failed to improve a model over its own base weights, since ordering
unmoved models would order their base behaviour under another name. It did not fire: all fifteen
models gained between $+0.041$ and $+0.153$.

\paragraph{A second recipe.} Both readers of an earlier draft raised the same objection: the
fine-tune reused the selector's own recipe, so a model that adapts quickly under it could win both.
Recipe B changes every setting the two shared (LoRA rank $64$, $\alpha{=}128$, learning rate
$3\times10^{-4}$, effective batch $16$, $400$ steps, a new seed) on the same task, data, decoding and
metric, preregistered before it ran. It fits the training articles far harder and scores slightly
below recipe A for every model, yet orders the fifteen systems almost identically (Spearman $0.982$).
Against recipe-B systems adapted BPB remains the top selector at $0.923$ $[0.750, 1.000]$, ahead of
HellaSwag at $0.846$, parameter count at $0.744$ and GSM8K and MMLU-Pro near chance, and it picks the
better system on all $20$ pairs recipe B resolves. Its margin over parameter count, resolved under
recipe A, does not resolve here ($+0.179$ $[-0.036, +0.441]$), so that margin should be read as
recipe-specific (Table~\ref{tab:downstream}).

\paragraph{What copying buys.} A news body restates its own lede, so reference overlap is partly
available by retrieval. Two reference points bound it: the article's first body sentence scores
$0.125$ against the reference and a random body sentence $0.111$. Unadapted base weights land close
to copying (inspection confirms they largely do copy), while every fine-tuned system clears it,
from $0.188$ (Qwen-2.5-0.5B) to $0.249$ (Gemma-4-12B).


\paragraph{How close the corpus has to be.} Exploratory, run after the result: scoring the news fine-tune with adapted BPB
from each corpus, on the ten size-matched pairs of the nine fine-tuned models that have all four,
gives $0.900$ for news and for Reddit, $0.700$ for Hacker News and $0.600$ for arXiv mathematics; the
unadapted readings reach $0.500$--$0.600$. Fresh general text carries the prediction, and
mathematics, which aims the ranking at reasoning (Section~\ref{sec:agreement}), carries it least. Ten pairs support no interval, so this is a direction, not a result.

\paragraph{What this does not show.} One task, one domain, two recipes, one seed each. On this task adapted
BPB ordered the fine-tuned systems better than any other selector and GSM8K and MMLU-Pro ordered them
at chance; its margin over HellaSwag does not resolve. It is not a claim that adapted BPB predicts
downstream utility in general, and the paper does not make one.

\section{The Benchmark Column Re-Score}
\label{app:rescore}
\paragraph{Harness configuration.} MMLU-Pro is 5-shot chain-of-thought with exact match on one
stratified $1{,}000$-question subset (seed 42); HellaSwag is 10-shot length-normalised
multiple-choice accuracy on all $10{,}042$ validation examples; GSM8K is 5-shot chain-of-thought with
flexible numeric extraction on all $1{,}319$ test examples. All bf16, greedy, tokenizer-native BOS.

July's eleven-model column was scored on a host that no longer exists and generative scores do not
pool across hosts: identical released weights move $-0.60$ to $+0.69$ GSM8K points when only the
host changes, A100 to H100, so it was re-scored in full: seventeen models $\times$ three benchmarks $=$
fifty-one cells, one host (A100-SXM-64GB), one build, with the design and the exclusion rule fixed
in advance. Task definitions match exactly ($17/17$ identical task hashes on both hashed benchmarks)
and every overlapping cell agrees with July to within $1.3$ points, most to within $0.3$.

Reaching that took one correction worth reporting, because the fault was in our own fix. Because the
newer backend honours each checkpoint's shipped \texttt{generation\_config.json} and the original did
not, we replaced every checkpoint's generation settings with one identical policy, which also
discarded \texttt{suppress\_tokens}, a setting that is live for gemma-4-12B. That single omission cost
it $9.86$ GSM8K and $14.30$ MMLU-Pro points while leaving HellaSwag, scored by likelihood and never
calling \texttt{generate()}, intact. Keeping the setting for all seventeen models reproduces July to
$+1.21$ points on GSM8K, $+0.10$ on MMLU-Pro and $-0.27$ on HellaSwag. That GSM8K cell is the one
outside the preregistered $1.0$-point tolerance, within the allowance of one that the re-score
declared in advance: the column is within its stated allowance rather than clean, and those are
different claims. The lesson generalises: a wrapper imposed to remove a confound can be the confound, so
the unmodified harness belongs in the design as a control.
