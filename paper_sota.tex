\title{\LARGE \textbf{Back to Entropy: Evaluating Base Language Models\\ Beyond Perplexity and Benchmarks}}
\author{David Afonso Valente\\\normalsize Bottlecap AI}

\date{}

\begin{document}

\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
A language model is trained to predict text and should be judged by how well it predicts the text
it will be used on. Base models are instead chosen by public benchmarks, which vendors optimise for,
which can leak into training data, and which score a model before the adaptation every team applies.
We return to the model's own objective: adapt every candidate to fresh, decontaminated domain text by
the same procedure and rank by held-out bits per byte---no labels, minutes of GPU time per model with
a fixed recipe. Adaptation changes the ordering. With no ground truth to appeal to, we compare the
new ordering with independent views of the models---fine-tuning fifteen of them on a news task, and
quizzes built from four corpora. Each benchmark falls short on some view; adapted bits per byte
agrees with every one at least as closely as any benchmark, matching the fine-tuned outcome in
$0.949$ of size-matched pairs. It also tracks HellaSwag and turns toward the reasoning benchmarks on
mathematics. Code and artifacts are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Everything a base model can do, it learned by predicting the next piece of text. The most direct way
to ask how good one is, for a given use, is therefore to measure how well it predicts the text of
that use. That is not how base models are chosen. A new one ships most weeks, and what arrives with it is a
scorecard---MMLU-Pro, HellaSwag, GSM8K---read as the answer to \emph{how good is this model, for what
we do?} This paper argues that language models should be evaluated as language models: by the loss they
were trained to minimise, on the text they will be used on. No task-specific score can serve as
ground truth for a base model, which is chosen before most of its uses exist; the case for loss is
that it is the one quantity every use is built on, and the evidence is that it agrees with every concrete use we could test at least as closely as any
benchmark, while each benchmark falls short on some.

The scorecard misleads for two reasons. The first is that benchmarks are public. Each was written by
other people, for other purposes, and released before the model existed; vendors optimise for the
ones they report, and their items and subject matter can reach pre-training data. A high score is
therefore consistent with a genuinely stronger model or with one that saw more of that exam, and
because pre-training mixtures are not disclosed, no reader can tell which. Inside our own cohort,
Llama-3.2-1B and Qwen2.5-1.5B are
$2.1$ points apart on HellaSwag and $3.4\%$ apart in adapted bits per byte, yet $55.3$ points apart
on GSM8K: two models that model fresh English almost identically well differ by nearly a factor of
ten on one exam. The second reason is timing. Teams rarely deploy a base model exactly as it ships;
they adapt it to their data first, so what matters is how good a model \emph{becomes}. A model that
has simply never seen prose like yours can look weak out of the box and still overtake one that
looked stronger. Gemma-4-12B is the case in point: of fifteen models it is ninth on GSM8K, eighth on
MMLU-Pro and thirteenth on raw predictive loss, and it builds the best fine-tuned system of all
(Table~\ref{tab:outofbox}).

There is no universal ground truth for which base model is ``better''; any yardstick is a choice,
and loss is the principled one. Every base model was trained to minimise cross-entropy on text, so held-out loss
is each model's own objective measured out of sample, and every other ability it has was learned
through that objective. Cross-entropy is also a proper scoring rule: in expectation over text drawn from the
domain's distribution $p$, a model $q$ scores $H(p,q)=H(p)+\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q)$, and $H(p)$ is the
same for every model, so the difference between two models' expected losses is \emph{exactly} the
difference in how far each is from the distribution that produced the text; held-out loss estimates
it \cite{shannon1951prediction}. Taking that seriously asks three things of the measurement, and
each answers one of the failures above. The loss is counted in \emph{bytes}, because every model
counts in its own tokens and bytes are the one unit they all share. It is computed on \emph{fresh,
decontaminated} text---dated after every candidate shipped, deduplicated, and screened for
membership---so that no candidate has trained on it. And it follows \emph{equal adaptation}:
every candidate is adapted to the domain by the same procedure---its own hyperparameter search, then
training to convergence---which turns ``which model already writes like my corpus'' into ``which
model becomes best at it'' and so measures each model after adaptation, as it will be used
(Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}). The protocol needs only the domain's raw text, no labels, and minutes of
GPU time per model. Train-before-test \cite{zhang2025trainbeforetest} also prepares candidates
identically before comparing them, but with labelled benchmark data; we do it unsupervised, on fresh
text, and in bytes.

We test the protocol on seventeen base models from eight families (0.5B--35B) and four corpora, and
the results form one argument. \textbf{Adaptation changes the ordering.} Against a fill-in-the-blank quiz on held-out news, eleven of fifty-five model pairs move from
disagreeing to agreeing with it after adaptation and none the other way, because the unadapted number mixes familiarity with this prose into
capability and adaptation largely removes the familiarity (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).
\textbf{The new ordering recovers what benchmarks measure, and can be aimed.} Over eleven models it
matches HellaSwag at Spearman $0.96$--$0.98$ on news, Reddit and Hacker News, up from $0.63$--$0.72$
before adaptation, and when the text is mathematics it swings to GSM8K and MMLU-Pro ($0.91$ and
$0.93$; Section~\ref{sec:agreement}). \textbf{It anticipates a real use it never saw.} Measured before any task exists, it matches the
ordering of fifteen models fine-tuned on a news task in $0.949$ of size-matched pairs (within
$2\times$ in parameters)---more than any benchmark (HellaSwag $0.872$; GSM8K and MMLU-Pro near a
coin)---and places every model within one rank of where it finishes
(Section~\ref{sec:divergence}). \textbf{And it holds.} Over seventeen models and four corpora, against a quiz built from each
corpus, it never agrees less than a public benchmark does (Section~\ref{sec:pairwise}). Section~\ref{sec:protocol} gives the protocol and the independent views we compare it with, Section~\ref{sec:results} the results in this order, and
Section~\ref{sec:using} how to use them. Every number recomputes from a committed artifact:
\url{https://github.com/DavidAfonsoValente/entropy-bench}.

\section{The Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
The protocol has two halves, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}: the measurement itself, and the
independent views of the models we compare it with.

\subsection{The measurement}
\label{sec:phases}
The measurement runs in three phases, and the order is load-bearing: the corpus is fixed and
screened before any model touches it.

\paragraph{Phase 1: a decontaminated corpus.} Evaluation text must be text no candidate has seen,
and we guarantee it three ways. \emph{Time:} collect text from the target domain dated after every
candidate's release---Reddit and Hacker News are scraped in August 2026, after every model in the
cohort shipped, and the arXiv set runs from 9 June to 20 August. \emph{Hygiene:} deduplicate exactly,
filter with Gopher quality heuristics, remove MinHash/LSH near-duplicates at Jaccard $0.60$, and split
80/10/10 with no identifier or normalised-text overlap across splits, so nothing in the test split
reappears in training. \emph{Membership screens:} for the news snapshot (Google News, 8 June 2026,
$119{,}054$ articles), which predates part of the release window, every model is run through three
reference-free membership tests---Min-K++ \cite{zhang2024minkpp}, sensitivity to in-corpus context,
and a perturbation test---and the \emph{union} of everything any model flags is quarantined, so every
candidate is measured on one identical retained corpus (Appendix~\ref{app:criterion}).

\paragraph{Phase 2: equal adaptation.} Every candidate is adapted to the retained training text,
packed into contiguous $512$-token blocks, with LoRA \cite{hu2021lora} on all linear layers, and
every candidate is adapted by the same procedure. Its hyperparameters come from its own Optuna search
\cite{akiba2019optuna}: a TPE sampler proposes the learning rate, batch size, warm-up, weight decay,
LoRA rank, $\alpha$ and dropout; successive halving prunes weak trials from $50$ steps on; and every
trial is scored by BPB on the validation split, never the test split. Every model receives the same
number of trials: a search capped by wall-clock time instead lets small models finish more trials
than large ones and so tunes them better, a size bias we observed directly
(Appendix~\ref{app:robust}). The chosen configuration is then trained until validation BPB stops
improving, so each candidate is measured near the best it can reach on the domain. The released
pipeline implements this procedure, and accepts a hand-picked configuration in place of the search.

Searching seventeen models of up to 35B parameters in full was beyond our compute budget, so the
results reported here use that second path: one hand-picked configuration for every candidate---LoRA
rank 16, $\alpha{=}32$, dropout $0.05$, learning rate $10^{-4}$, effective batch 32---and a fixed $250$
update steps rather than training to convergence. That is $4.1$M training tokens per candidate, which
on one A100 cost $3.7$ GPU-minutes at $0.5$B, $11.6$ at $7.7$B, $24.7$ at $12$B and $62.7$ at $31$B.
The configuration sits where our own earlier search settled---rank 16 won for eight of the eleven
models and the median winning learning rate was $1.1\times10^{-4}$ (Appendix~\ref{app:robust})---but
it suits no architecture perfectly, and the full procedure can only tune each candidate closer to its
best.

\paragraph{Phase 3: bits per byte.} Compute cross-entropy on the held-out test split and divide by
the UTF-8 byte length $|B|$ of the text those predictions cover:
\begin{equation}
\bpb = \frac{\loss_{\text{nats}}}{\ln 2} \times \frac{|\text{Tokens}|}{|B|}
\label{eq:bpb}
\end{equation}
The byte count is a property of the text and identical for every candidate, so a larger vocabulary
earns no advantage the way it would under per-token perplexity. Test blocks are independent and
non-overlapping, so context resets at every cut, and injected document-start markers stay available
as context but are always masked as prediction targets. We report both endpoints, before adaptation
and after, because the difference between them is itself informative (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).

\paragraph{Cohort and setup.} We evaluate 11 base checkpoints spanning dense Transformers, one
sparse MoE and one Liquid architecture (0.5B--35B parameters; Table~\ref{tab:leaderboard}), extended
to 17 in Section~\ref{sec:pairwise}, from the Llama, Qwen, Gemma, Ministral, LiquidAI, Falcon,
Mistral and SmolLM families
\cite{dubey2024llama3,yang2024qwen25,gemma2026gemma4,liu2026ministral3,qwen2026qwen35}. For
comparison every model is also scored on MMLU-Pro \cite{wang2024mmlupro}, HellaSwag
\cite{zellers2019hellaswag} and GSM8K \cite{cobbe2021gsm8k} with \emph{lm-evaluation-harness} 0.4.12
\cite{gao2024lmeval} on one host and one build, under the protocol of
Appendix~\ref{app:rescore}---directly observed scores rather than vendor-reported card values, with
the harness logs published so each recomputes from raw output.
Adaptation ran on Leonardo (CINECA), 3 nodes $\times$ 4 A100 64\,GB. Intervals never resample
pairs, because pairs that share a model are not independent: a \emph{selector's} accuracy resamples
\textbf{models} ($20{,}000$ draws, percentile, clustered), the \emph{criterion's own} scores its
$2{,}500$ \textbf{items}, paired across models, and a fine-tuned pair its $500$ evaluation articles.

\subsection{Independent views, not ground truth}
\label{sec:criterion}
No measurement of a base model can be ground truth, because a base model is chosen before most of
its uses exist and each use can order the models differently. A task shows which model is better
\emph{at that task}; GSM8K and HellaSwag disagree precisely because they are different tasks. The
case for adapted BPB therefore rests on the principle of Section~\ref{sec:intro}, and what can be
tested is whether a quantity chosen on principle agrees with concrete uses it was never shown.
We build two such views, neither of which uses a public benchmark, and throughout the paper a
selector is ``right'' on a pair when it orders the pair as the view does.

The first is one real use: fine-tune every model on a news task and score the systems that come out
(Section~\ref{sec:divergence}). If that task were the only one that mattered, fine-tuning on it would
be the better evaluation; it is expensive, needs labels, and speaks for one task, so we run it once, on
fifteen models, as a use the measurement is asked to anticipate. The second is cheap and repeatable: a fill-in-the-blank quiz built from each corpus's held-out test
split: hide a name or a number in an article no model was adapted on, show the model the $1{,}200$
characters before it, and count how often it writes the missing words (Appendix~\ref{app:criterion}
shows an item). A hidden span that already appears earlier in the prompt is excluded, so a right
answer means knowing, not copying. We build one quiz per corpus and call the ranking it produces
\emph{the criterion}. A base model has no convention for stopping, so after the answer it often keeps
going, or opens with a word of its own; \emph{strict} scoring therefore counts an answer only if the
generation begins with it, and \emph{lenient} scoring if it begins within the first $24$ characters.
Lenient is our primary convention, because it scores what the model knows rather than how it stops.
We report both, and every comparison with a public benchmark holds under both: adapted BPB never ranks
below one under either. On news, the corpus where we ran both checks, the quiz and the fine-tune rank the
fifteen models at Spearman $0.921$ (lenient), so on news the two views largely agree, and the quiz carries the comparison to the other three
corpora.

The quiz is a fair judge for three reasons. It cannot have been prepared for: Reddit and Hacker News
were scraped after every model's weights were frozen, most of the arXiv set was written after them
too, and the news snapshot is protected by the membership screens of Phase~1
(Table~\ref{tab:corpora}). It is scored by generation and string match rather than likelihood, which
puts distance between it and BPB; HellaSwag, by contrast, ranks continuations by length-normalised
conditional log-likelihood---the same object BPB measures---so agreement between BPB and any
likelihood-scored criterion would be convergent validity rather than evidence, though greedy decoding
is still the argmax of the same conditional. And, most importantly, it is answered by the released
weights, not the adapted ones: whatever the criterion and BPB share, they cannot share the
adaptation under test.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
The results follow the four steps of the argument. The \textbf{original eleven models} have both BPB
readings on all four corpora and carry Sections~\ref{sec:mechanism} and~\ref{sec:agreement} and
the four-corpus comparison of Section~\ref{sec:pairwise} ($55$ pairs, $10$--$11$ within $2\times$ in
size); the fine-tune uses the fifteen of the \textbf{extended seventeen} that fit one GPU, and the
size-matched comparison of Section~\ref{sec:pairwise} all seventeen. Each table names its population.

\subsection{Adaptation changes the ordering}
\label{sec:mechanism}
Adapting every model before measuring does not just lower every score; it reorders the models, toward
the criterion. Table~\ref{tab:mechanism} puts each model's rank under the criterion beside its rank
under the two BPB readings. Ranking by the adapted number reproduces the criterion's ordering almost
exactly: seven of eleven models land in precisely the right position and the mean rank error is
$0.36$ places. The unadapted reading places two of eleven correctly and is off by $2.00$ places.
Eleven of the fifty-five model pairs flip from wrongly to correctly ordered when the reading changes,
and none flips the other way. That gap is not an artefact of a noisy yardstick: resampling the
criterion's own items while holding both BPB orderings fixed leaves the adapted reading at $0.36$
$[0.00, 0.55]$ places against $2.00$ $[2.00, 2.18]$ for the unadapted one, and the difference
between them, $1.64$ $[1.45, 2.00]$, excludes zero. These are lenient ranks; under strict scoring
the two readings tie in the size-matched band ($0.850$ each on the seventeen-model cohort, $0.727$
on the eleven), so the case for adapting rests on the mechanism as much as on the count.

The mechanism is familiarity. Zero-shot loss conflates two things a practitioner needs separated:
\emph{this model already writes like my corpus} and \emph{this model is a capable model}.
Figure~\ref{fig:blockpos} shows the confound in the raw loss, resolved by position inside a packed
evaluation block. Because blocks are independent, every block is a cold start. Unadapted,
Gemma-4-12B opens a block at $2.83$ bits per byte where Qwen-2.5-1.5B---eight times smaller---opens
at $2.04$, and it stays behind for roughly the first half of the block; by the end of the block it has
passed it, $0.72$ against $0.77$. A block-averaged number therefore reports mostly how quickly a model
finds its footing in unfamiliar prose, which is a real property but not the one the decision turns
on. A short equal adaptation supplies exactly the missing familiarity, and what is left is closer to
capability. If that is right, the models the unadapted reading most under-rates should be the ones
adaptation helps most, and they are: the three largest zero-shot-to-adapted reductions in the cohort
are Gemma-4-12B, Gemma-4-31B and LFM2.5-1.2B, which are also the three the free reading most
under-rates. Across the cohort the correlation between a model's adaptation gain and its zero-shot
rank error is $0.672$; against its \emph{adapted} rank error it is $0.024$. The confound shows up in
the unadapted number and not in the adapted one.

The cold start is part of the story, not all of it. Scoring every model zero-shot while discarding
the first $K$ positions of each block lifts in-band accuracy from $0.545$ to at best $0.636$ at
$K{=}128$, a quarter of the way to $0.909$, the adapted reading's score on the same eleven models, so
the criterion and adapted BPB do not merely meet in a warmed-up regime (Appendix~\ref{app:robust}).
Two models show the effect at its sharpest. \textbf{Gemma-4-12B} is ninth of eleven on zero-shot BPB
but second on both the criterion and adapted BPB---a $12$B model the free reading puts below two
models under $2$B. \textbf{LFM2.5-1.2B} is the same effect at the other end: dead last zero-shot at
$1.34$ bits per byte against a $0.5$B Qwen's $0.93$, yet ahead of it after adaptation and on the
criterion---a non-Transformer penalised hardest by a metric that rewards familiarity.

\subsection{The new ordering recovers what benchmarks measure, and can be aimed}
\label{sec:agreement}
A new measurement should first recover what the existing ones get right. Of the three public
benchmarks, HellaSwag is the one closest to language modelling: it asks which ending of a passage is
most natural, and the harness scores it by the likelihood of that ending. Before adaptation, BPB on
news, Reddit and Hacker News ranks the eleven models at Spearman $0.63$--$0.72$ with HellaSwag;
after adaptation the same rank correlation is $0.96$--$0.98$, and on news the adapted ranking orders
$53$ of HellaSwag's $55$ pairs the same way (Table~\ref{tab:alignment}; benchmark scores in
Table~\ref{tab:static}). The effect is specific to what the text is about. On general text the
correlation with GSM8K falls on all three corpora, and with MMLU-Pro on two of them; adapted on arXiv
mathematics instead, BPB swings toward the reasoning benchmarks---Spearman $0.91$ with GSM8K and
$0.93$ with MMLU-Pro, up from $0.76$ and $0.74$---so the adaptation corpus steers which capability
the ranking tracks. Because HellaSwag and BPB are both likelihoods, this is consistency rather than
validation: measuring on fresh text of one's own gives up nothing the likelihood benchmark captures,
and the choice of text decides which capability the ranking follows. Whether it also anticipates a real use of the models is the next question.

\subsection{It anticipates a real use it never saw}
\label{sec:divergence}
A measurement made with no task and no labels is most useful if it anticipates how models fare when
a task does arrive. So we gave the models one, trained them on it, and asked how often each way of
choosing, fixed beforehand, orders the resulting systems as the task does.

\section{Using the Measurement}
\label{sec:using}
In practice the measurement slots into a short procedure. When candidates differ by more than about
$2\times$ in parameters, the larger one is usually the better choice; size is a good rule of thumb
there, though adapted BPB beats it even across the full $70\times$ range under lenient scoring, and
the protocol earns its cost where size has stopped deciding. Otherwise the text comes from the target
domain, or close to it, dated after every candidate shipped and screened as in Phase~1; the smallest
corpus we report on is $15$\,MB, and nearby text can serve too---on the ten pairs tested, Reddit BPB
predicted the news fine-tune as well as news BPB did (Appendix~\ref{sec:downstream}). Every finalist is
then adapted by the same procedure and ranked by adapted BPB: the per-model search where compute
allows, the hand-picked recipe where it does not, which at the costs of Phase~2 is cheap enough to run
on every candidate rather than a shortlist; assembling the corpus, not the GPU time, is
the main cost. The unadapted number is best read as a free screen, not the selector: it flags a
candidate badly off-distribution for the target text, but alone reaches only $0.545$ in the
size-matched band (lenient; under strict the two readings are level).

With two or three candidates rather than a cohort, the \emph{gap} is what matters, and we treat
anything under $2\%$ as a tie. A different seed moved adapted BPB by up to $1.24\%$ (Gemma-4-12B; the median model,
$0.01\%$) and token-aligned blocks by $0.18\%$ (Appendix~\ref{app:robust}). Of the $33$
size-matched pairs at least $2\%$ apart, the lower-BPB model was the criterion's preference in
\textbf{all $33$} lenient and $30$ of $33$ strict; closer than that, licence, latency or serving cost
should decide. The threshold was chosen after seeing this cohort, so Table~\ref{tab:decision} is a
calibration rather than an estimate. Results are comparable only when corpus, split, context, masking
and adaptation all match.

\section{Conclusion}
Language models should be evaluated as language models: by the loss they were trained to minimise,
in bits per byte, on fresh decontaminated text from the domain of use, after the same adaptation for
every candidate. There is no ground truth to appeal to; what can be checked is whether the principled quantity agrees with the concrete uses we could build,
and it agrees with each at least as closely as any benchmark, while each benchmark falls short on
some.
Adaptation changes the ordering; the new ordering agrees with HellaSwag at Spearman $0.96$--$0.98$ on
general text and turns to the reasoning benchmarks on mathematics; it matches the outcome of fine-tuning fifteen models more often than any benchmark, each within one
rank of its finish; and on four
corpora it never ranks candidates worse than a public benchmark. It needs only raw text and minutes of
GPU time, runs on fresh screened text no benchmark can offer, and captures what a benchmark structurally cannot: how good a
model becomes once adapted.

The natural objections were tested. \emph{Why not simply fine-tune on the task?} When the task, its
labels and the budget exist, that is the better evaluation for that task; a base model is usually
chosen before any of them do, and must serve many tasks at once. \emph{Circularity}---a yardstick built from the same corpus
rewarding the method---is answered by the fine-tune, scored on separate documents against
human-written references, whose ranking agrees with the criterion at Spearman $0.921$. \emph{Recipe
dependence} is answered by repeating every fine-tune with a substantially different recipe, which
leaves the ranking nearly unchanged (Spearman $0.982$). \emph{Contamination} is addressed by dating,
deduplication and membership screens, and \emph{selection bias} by extending the cohort on preregistered
criteria and scoring every size-matched pair. What remains is power: at seventeen models the lead over HellaSwag is
consistent but not yet resolved, and a larger cohort is the next step.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\textbf{From perplexity to comparable loss.} Language modelling was grounded in byte- and
character-level entropy from the start \cite{shannon1951prediction}, but token-level perplexity
depends on each model's vocabulary \cite{mielke2019perplexity,ahia2023languages}; normalising by
UTF-8 bytes restores a common unit \cite{gao2020pile,magnusson2023paloma}, and loss-based metrics
carry better signal and lower noise than accuracy across 465 models \cite{heineman2025signal}. Our
contribution is the operational protocol around the metric, not the metric.

\textbf{Where the loss is measured matters.} Thrush et al.\ turn the varying correlation between
per-domain perplexity and benchmark performance into a pretraining-data selection rule
\cite{thrush2024perplexity}. Their estimand is the complement of ours: they fix the benchmark suite
and choose data to \emph{train} one strong model, where we fix the candidate pool and ask how the
\emph{selection ranking} moves.

\textbf{Equal preparation.} Dynamic evaluation updates a model on recent test history
\cite{krause2018dynamic,krause2019dynamictransformer} and domain-adaptive pretraining improves fit to
a target distribution \cite{gururangan2020dapt}. Closest to our motivation, \emph{train-before-test}
gives 61 models identical supervised preparation across 24 tasks and raises cross-benchmark Kendall
agreement from $0.52$ to $0.76$ \cite{zhang2025trainbeforetest}; they rank post-fine-tuning accuracy
on public labelled benchmarks, where we rank held-out fit after \emph{unsupervised} adaptation to a
fresh corpus, across tokenizers. The core idea---prepare every candidate identically before
comparing them---is theirs; what we add is doing it without labels, on text no vendor has seen, in a
unit that compares tokenizers, and checking the result against real fine-tuned systems. Controlled
families show a smooth loss-to-error relation
\cite{gadre2025scales,ruan2024observational}; we test it \emph{across} families.

\textbf{Contamination.} Models memorise duplicated pretraining text
\cite{carlini2021extracting,carlini2022quantifying}, so deduplication and contamination analysis are
necessary \cite{lee2022deduplicating,xu2024survey}; reference-free membership signals are useful
screens \cite{shi2023mink,zhang2024minkpp} but prove nothing about a proprietary training set, so we
use them only for conservative quarantine.

\section{The Criterion: Construction and Composition}
\label{app:criterion}
Table~\ref{tab:criterion} gives the four criteria. Items come from each corpus's own held-out test
split, reproduced with the evaluation pipeline's shuffle and seed. Each hides one entity or number
span and supplies $1{,}200$ characters of preceding context; spans occurring earlier in the prompt
are dropped, so retrieval from context cannot substitute for prediction. Generation is greedy, scored
by string match, and produced by the released base weights.

Composition differs across the four, which is why we never compare their levels: news is
entity-dominated and Reddit number-dominated, and numeric spans are simply easier---answered at
$0.279$ and $0.325$ against $0.180$ and $0.060$ for entity spans on those two corpora. Within a
corpus every selector is scored on identical items, which is the only comparison the paper draws.

A worked item, answered by two of the cohort:

\begin{center}\small
\fbox{\parbox{0.94\columnwidth}{\ldots\ MBDA is owned by Airbus ($37.5\%$), BAE Systems ($37.5\%$), and Leonardo
(\underline{\hspace{2em}}\\[2pt]
\emph{hidden span:} \texttt{25\%}\quad
\emph{Gemma-4-12B:} \texttt{25\%).} \checkmark\quad
\emph{Qwen-2.5-0.5B:} \texttt{30\%).} $\times$}}
\end{center}

\noindent The answer is nowhere in the context; the model has to know the company, or
do the sum.

\paragraph{How precisely is the quiz known?} We resample its $2{,}500$ items, paired across models
so that item difficulty cancels out of a between-model difference. It separates the cohort from
$0.066$ to $0.213$ and resolves seven of the ten adjacent pairs under lenient scoring, two under
strict; Table~\ref{tab:mechanism} carries every interval. Adjacent models it cannot order are
reported as unordered rather than counted as some selector's mistake.

\paragraph{Does sharing text with BPB make it circular?} The criterion and the BPB evaluation
do come from the same held-out bytes. The evidence that this
does not drive the result is internal: the reading \emph{mechanically closest} to the
criterion---zero-shot BPB, same weights, same text, same conditional---is the weaker of the two at
$0.545$, while the adapted reading, from different weights, is the best. Shared bytes alone do not
buy agreement, and Section~\ref{sec:divergence} leaves the loop altogether: a real fine-tune, scored
on different documents against a human-written reference.

\paragraph{Is it just measuring size?} Such a criterion could also be a size proxy. Over the full $70\times$ range it does track size
($\rho = 0.918$ against log parameters), as any capability measure should. \emph{Inside} the band,
parameter count clears chance against none of the four per-corpus criteria, though it does on the
extended news cohort under strict ($0.750$ $[0.517, 0.932]$), and two dense size inversions
resolve---Gemma-4-12B over the larger Ministral-3-14B, $+0.044$ $[+0.032, +0.057]$, and Llama-3.2-1B
over Qwen-2.5-1.5B, $+0.016$ $[+0.006, +0.026]$. (The one mixture-of-experts model
activates $3$B of $34.7$B and is never used as a size example.)

\paragraph{Screening.} The news corpus additionally passes three reference-free membership screens
per model---Min-K++ (normalised likelihood of the lowest-probability $K{=}20\%$ target tokens),
context sensitivity (target NLL with and without sampled same-corpus prefixes), and, for flagged
candidates only, preference over two word-swap/placeholder perturbations. The global union
quarantined $80$ unique sequences, after which every model receives the identical retained corpus.
Near-$100\%$ preference for the unperturbed original is expected---fluency alone produces it---so we
draw no memorisation conclusion from it (\texttt{results/contamination/}).

\section{Robustness and Measurement Artefacts}
\label{app:robust}

\paragraph{Stability across domains.}\label{sec:domains} All eleven models ran on three independently collected domains under the same recipe
(Tables~\ref{tab:corpora} and~\ref{tab:leaderboard}). Adaptation genuinely changes the
choice---zero-shot and adapted rankings agree only at Spearman $\rho = 0.655$ on news, $0.609$ on
Reddit and $0.682$ on Hacker News, with models moving up to seven places---and the adapted ordering
then holds across domains: news and Reddit agree on nine of eleven exact ranks
($\rho = 0.991$, maximum displacement one), news against Hacker News gives $0.955$ and Reddit
against Hacker News $0.964$, and the agreement is not merely ordinal (Figure~\ref{fig:crosscorpus}).
\textbf{So does the zero-shot ordering}---$0.982$, $0.964$ and $0.973$ on the same three
comparisons. The tempting story is that adaptation buys stability, and
it does not; it buys a \emph{different} stable ordering, and Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} is the
evidence that it is the better of the two.

\paragraph{Why the search gives every model the same number of trials.} The search of
Phase~2 uses a multivariate TPE sampler (seed $42$) over learning rate ($10^{-6}$--$10^{-3}$, log),
physical batch ($2$, $4$, $8$), gradient accumulation ($1$--$16$), warm-up ratio ($0$--$0.2$), weight
decay ($10^{-5}$--$10^{-1}$, log), LoRA rank ($8$--$64$), $\alpha$ as a multiple of rank ($1$, $2$,
$4$) and dropout ($0$--$0.2$); a successive-halving pruner starts trials at $50$ steps and promotes the
best third at each rung; and the objective is BPB on a fixed $256$-block subset of the validation
split. An earlier run of this search on the eleven-model news cohort was capped by wall-clock time
rather than by trial count, and the cap was confounded with model size: the two largest models
completed four trials each while the 1B models completed 17 and 18
(\texttt{results/legacy\_sweep/*/trials.csv}), and the five models of 9B or more all kept the same
third draw of the seeded sampler as their winner, while the smaller models' winners came from much
later in their searches. A time-capped search hands small models a better-tuned adapter and biases
the ranking by parameter count, which is why the released pipeline budgets the search in trials and
applies no time cap by default. None of those adapters produces a number in the paper; the reported
results use the hand-picked configuration of Phase~2.

\paragraph{The cold start, seen a second way.} Varying the inference context on $64$ fixed held-out
target windows from $16$ to $2048$ tokens reproduces Figure~\ref{fig:blockpos}'s effect
non-positionally: both models improve with context and the larger improves faster, so any protocol
that scores short contexts under-rates the models that use long ones. Measured context sensitivity is
$0.032$ bits per byte per doubling, which is the constant the block-alignment bound below uses.

\paragraph{Token-aligned blocks: a real bias, bounded.} Evaluation blocks are 512 \emph{tokens}
rather than a fixed byte span, so models with different tokenizers score slightly different amounts
of text---5.92--6.24\,MB across the eleven-model cohort, a $5\%$ spread, and $9.9\%$ across the
seventeen. Bounding the resulting bias with the measured context sensitivity puts it at
$0.08\times$ the smallest adjacent rank gap in the eleven-model cohort. In the extended cohort the
bound is looser and worth stating: on the worst adjacent pair, Qwen2.5-3B over Falcon3-7B, whose BPB
gap is only $0.29\%$, the bound reaches $0.61\times$ the gap. It still \textbf{overturns no rank},
but byte-aligned blocking would remove the question, and we recommend it for future work.

\paragraph{The resolved comparison is not an artifact of a lucky seed.} Every adaptation cell ran
with the \texttt{torch} RNG unseeded, and the cluster bootstrap resamples models, so it carries no
within-cell training variance. A preregistered check---three seed replicates for all $17$ models,
$51$ cells, news corpus, everything else pinned---is confirmatory on all four questions it asked.
Median within-model SD of adapted BPB is $0.000087$ against a between-model SD of $0.074340$, a
ratio of $0.0012$; in-band pairwise accuracy is $0.900$ under each seed; \emph{no} in-band
pair---$0$ of $40$---reverses its ordering; and adding a seed-resampling stage moves the resolved
interval by $0.001$. The spread is not uniform: Gemma-4-12B is the one model where seed noise is
visible at all (SD $0.00315$, $5.3\times$ the next-largest spread).

\paragraph{Steering: direction, not magnitude.} Adapting on general text raises agreement with
HellaSwag on news from $\rho = 0.718$ to $0.982$ while GSM8K falls from $0.800$ to $0.700$ and
MMLU-Pro from $0.845$ to $0.773$; adapting on contamination-screened arXiv mathematics instead
raises GSM8K agreement to $0.909$ and restores the GSM8K oracle pick at zero regret,
while three general-text corpora tried as controls all leave GSM8K at or below the do-nothing
baseline. That is the rotation, and it is why the body reports only its direction. Because the
steering hypothesis predicts a
\emph{joint} movement, the better-powered statistic is a difference in differences---(GSM8K minus
HellaSwag selection accuracy under mathematics adaptation) minus the same quantity under a
general-text corpus---which is $+0.242$ pooled. Resampling models gives a strictly positive
difference in $93.7\%$ of draws; the stricter publisher-family clustering the mechanism itself
implies gives $80.9\%$ with not one negative draw in $20{,}000$, but no longer clears $0.05$ on any
tie convention. A leave-one-family-out jackknife shows why: dropping the two Gemma models takes the
pooled difference from $+0.242$ to $+0.056$. The remedy is more \emph{families}, not more models.

\paragraph{Discarding the cold start: how much does it buy?} (E10.) Each model was re-scored
zero-shot with per-token loss accumulated separately at every position in the $512$-token block, so
every cutoff $K$ is derivable from one pass; the falsifying outcome was fixed in advance---if any $K$
brought the free reading within one in-band pair of adapted BPB, the mechanism claim above would
have been withdrawn. It does not. Under lenient scoring in-band accuracy rises from $0.545$ at
$K{=}0$ to $0.636$ at $K{=}128$ and no further, closing $25\%$ of the $0.364$ gap to adapted BPB.
Under strict scoring the test is \emph{uninformative} and we say so rather than counting it: the two
readings already tie at $0.727$ there, so there is no gap for the cold start to be accused of
causing, and discarding positions only makes the free reading worse ($0.636$ at $K{\geq}128$). The
re-measured cells are not the published ones---they run up to $1.5\%$ low---so every cutoff is
compared against this run's \emph{own} $K{=}0$, which reproduces the published zero-shot selector
exactly under both conventions.

\section{The Downstream Fine-Tune}
\label{sec:downstream}
Section~\ref{sec:divergence} reports the result; this is how it was built, and what it does and
does not license. It ran in two stages, each preregistered in code before its job returned: first the
two pairs on which the selectors disagree most (Gemma-4-12B against Ministral-3-14B, Llama-3.2-1B
against Qwen-2.5-1.5B), then every cohort model that fits one GPU, so that no pair is chosen by
anyone. Gemma-4-31B and Qwen-3.5-35B-MoE need more than one GPU \emph{for this fine-tune}---its
$1{,}280$-token sequences and its generation pass cost far more memory than the $512$-token
adaptation priced in Section~\ref{sec:intro}---and are excluded by that rule, which costs one in-band
pair of forty.

\paragraph{The task.} Journalistic convention makes a news article's first paragraph a standalone
summary of it, so the corpus supplies its own supervision: the input is the article with its opening
paragraph removed, truncated to $3{,}000$ characters, and the target is that paragraph. Articles
qualify if they have at least three paragraphs, an opening paragraph of $200$--$700$ characters
ending in sentence punctuation, and at least $800$ characters of remaining body. Of the $11{,}905$
validation documents, $3{,}627$ qualify; $2{,}000$ are sampled for training and $500$ for evaluation
under a fixed seed.

\paragraph{Why the validation split.} The in-domain criterion (Section~\ref{sec:criterion}) and the
BPB test blocks both live in the \emph{test} split. That is verified rather than assumed: the shipped
criterion items reproduce item-for-item from an independent reconstruction of the test split, and the
adaptation pipeline's contamination pass---which would have split the corpus differently---is off by
default and recorded as off in every published cell. Evaluation documents are then hashed against the
test documents twice, on full normalised text and on a $400$-character opening, the second because a
syndicated wire story is republished with an edited body and an identical lede. Four articles were
dropped by that second check and none by the first.

\paragraph{Equal preparation.} Both members of a pair get the paper's own adaptation recipe unchanged
---LoRA rank $16$, $\alpha{=}32$, dropout $0.05$, learning rate $10^{-4}$, effective batch $32$, $250$
steps---with prompt tokens masked out of the loss so the model is trained to write the lede rather
than to reconstruct the article. Nothing is tuned on either model, because a per-model search is
exactly the cost this protocol exists to avoid. The tokenizers differ, so the budget is matched in
update steps $\times$ batch $\times$ sequence length; each model's realised token count is in the
artifact. Decoding is greedy with a $180$-token cap and each checkpoint's own generation
configuration otherwise untouched, following Appendix~\ref{app:rescore}. The prompt is assembled
so that the instruction always survives truncation: the \emph{body} is cut to fit, never the
instruction after it, because right-truncating the assembled prompt would delete the instruction from
the longest articles at a different article count for each tokenizer---an asymmetry between the two
models being compared. At most four of the $500$ evaluation articles were cut for any model, and
none for three of them.

\paragraph{The decision rules, fixed before each run.} For the two chosen pairs: mean ROUGE-L F1
over the $500$ held-out articles, a paired bootstrap over articles ($20{,}000$ draws, $95\%$ percentile
interval) and a practical-equivalence margin of $0.01$---an interval excluding zero resolves the pair
for whichever reading predicted that winner. The first pair resolved for adapted BPB; the second was
inconclusive. For the full cohort: the fine-tuned ROUGE-L is treated as the criterion and every
selector is scored with the paper's own statistic---pairwise accuracy within $2\times$, model-level
cluster bootstrap, $20{,}000$ draws (Table~\ref{tab:downstream}). A separate guard voids any
comparison in which fine-tuning failed to improve a model over its own base weights, since ordering
unmoved models would order their base behaviour under another name. It did not fire: all fifteen
models gained between $+0.041$ and $+0.153$.

\paragraph{A second recipe.} Both readers of an earlier draft raised the same objection: the
fine-tune reused the selector's own recipe, so a model that adapts quickly under it could win both.
Recipe B changes every setting the two shared---LoRA rank $64$, $\alpha{=}128$, learning rate
$3\times10^{-4}$, effective batch $16$, $400$ steps, a new seed---on the same task, data, decoding and
metric, preregistered before it ran. It fits the training articles far harder and scores slightly
below recipe A for every model, yet orders the fifteen systems almost identically (Spearman $0.982$).
Against recipe-B systems adapted BPB remains the top selector at $0.923$ $[0.750, 1.000]$, ahead of
HellaSwag at $0.846$, parameter count at $0.744$ and GSM8K and MMLU-Pro near chance, and it picks the
better system on all $20$ pairs recipe B resolves. Its margin over parameter count, resolved under
recipe A, does not resolve here ($+0.179$ $[-0.036, +0.441]$), so that margin should be read as
recipe-specific (Table~\ref{tab:downstream}).

\paragraph{What copying buys.} A news body restates its own lede, so reference overlap is partly
available by retrieval. Two reference points bound it: the article's first body sentence scores
$0.125$ against the reference and a random body sentence $0.111$. Unadapted base weights land close
to copying---inspection confirms they largely do copy---while every fine-tuned system clears it,
from $0.188$ (Qwen-2.5-0.5B) to $0.249$ (Gemma-4-12B).

\paragraph{How close the corpus has to be.} Exploratory, run after the result at a reader's request:
scoring the news fine-tune with adapted BPB from each of the other corpora, on the ten in-band pairs

general text does most of the work and nearby text does it best. Ten pairs support no interval, so

\paragraph{What this does not show.} One task, one domain, two recipes, one seed each. On this task adapted
BPB ordered the fine-tuned systems better than any other selector and GSM8K and MMLU-Pro ordered them
at chance; its margin over HellaSwag does not resolve. It is not a claim that adapted BPB predicts
downstream utility in general, and the paper does not make one.

\section{The Benchmark Column Re-Score}
\label{app:rescore}
\paragraph{Harness configuration.} MMLU-Pro is 5-shot chain-of-thought with exact match on one
stratified $1{,}000$-question subset (seed 42); HellaSwag is 10-shot length-normalised
multiple-choice accuracy on all $10{,}042$ validation examples; GSM8K is 5-shot chain-of-thought with
flexible numeric extraction on all $1{,}319$ test examples. All bf16, greedy, tokenizer-native BOS.

July's eleven-model column was scored on a host that no longer exists and generative scores do not
pool across hosts---identical released weights move $-0.60$ to $+0.69$ GSM8K points when only the
host changes, A100 to H100---so it was re-scored in full---seventeen models $\times$ three benchmarks $=$
fifty-one cells, one host (A100-SXM-64GB), one build---with the design and the exclusion rule fixed
in advance. Task definitions match exactly ($17/17$ identical task hashes on both hashed benchmarks)
and every overlapping cell agrees with July to within $1.3$ points, most to within $0.3$.

Reaching that took one correction worth reporting, because the fault was in our own fix. Because the
newer backend honours each checkpoint's shipped \texttt{generation\_config.json} and the original did
not, we replaced every checkpoint's generation settings with one identical policy---which also
discarded \texttt{suppress\_tokens}, a setting that is live for gemma-4-12B. That single omission cost
it $9.86$ GSM8K and $14.30$ MMLU-Pro points while leaving HellaSwag, scored by likelihood and never
calling \texttt{generate()}, intact. Keeping the setting for all seventeen models reproduces July to
$+1.21$ points on GSM8K, $+0.10$ on MMLU-Pro and $-0.27$ on HellaSwag. That GSM8K cell is the one
outside the preregistered $1.0$-point tolerance, within the allowance of one that the re-score
declared in advance: the column is within its stated allowance rather than clean, and those are
different claims. The lesson generalises: a wrapper imposed to remove a confound can be the confound, so
the unmodified harness belongs in the design as a control.
